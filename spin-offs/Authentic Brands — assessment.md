# Authentic Brands — an assessment

## What the two documents are

There are two pieces of writing in front of us and they are at very different stages.

The first is a novel. Its working title is *Clones of Elvis*, and about five-sixths of it exists as finished prose. It is a comic satire set in an unnamed city that is recognisably Sydney, in a country called Ulyssia, at some unspecified point centuries from now. The city is stacked vertically: the rich live above a poisonous chemical cloud called the Layer, in crystalline air with visible stars, and everybody else lives underneath it in corrosive murk. Centuries ago a company called Grayling Corporation mass-produced cloned Elvis Presleys and sold them into hospitality and domestic service. The clones were eventually freed. Their descendants — the Elvi — now form a ghettoised underclass with their own parliament, and engage in a genuine and violent disagreement about what the lyrics are. The novel's plot concerns a woman named Sarah Jacobson who jokes about being "invincible", a Cloudborne cleaner named Tony "Chanta" Chantara who accidentally becomes the Elvi's messiah, an object called a Gyges Disc that shakes reality when you spin it, and three ancient con men called the Sages who are engineering the end of the universe on a schedule.

The second document is a proposal. It is titled *Authentic Brands*, and it describes itself in its own subtitle as "A provisional *Clones of Elvis* side-quest treatment." It is an outline in five acts, written from outside the novel — by someone working on a different project, proposing that two characters from that project cross into the *Clones of Elvis* world. Its premise is that Authentic Brands Group, the corporation which owns the commercial rights to Elvis Presley in our world, discovers that the clone universe exists, declares that its ownership follows Elvis across the boundary between universes, and sends investigators in to build a case.

This assessment does two things in order. First it takes *Authentic Brands* on its own terms and asks whether it holds together as the thing it is trying to be. Then it sets the proposal beside the novel and works out where the two agree, where they describe the same material in incompatible ways, and what has to be decided as a result.

Throughout, one distinction is kept strictly: what the finished prose actually establishes, and what exists only as a recorded decision about scenes not yet written. These are different kinds of fact and the difference matters enormously here, because several of the places where the proposal and the novel collide are places where the novel's own ending is a decision on paper rather than a page of prose.

## Part One: *Authentic Brands* on its own terms

### What it sets out to be

It wants to be "a legal-metaphysical caper with genuine stakes" — comedy built out of "the absurdity of cosmic intellectual-property enforcement, thousands of Elvises and bureaucracy conducted in rhinestones," but comedy that "should open onto serious questions of personhood, colonial authority, cultural transmission, religious practice and corporate ownership."

The question it puts at the centre is a good one, and it is worth quoting whole:

> Can a corporation own a living song after that song has become embodied by persons, transmitted as oral tradition, sanctified through pilgrimage and altered by time?

That is a real question. It has a real-world edge that needs no explaining to any reader who has watched a dead musician's catalogue change hands. And it is not an abstract question in the story, because the answer decides whether a few million singing people are persons or inventory.

The shape is a five-act legal story. A corporation discovers the clone world and values it. It proclaims a doctrine — "Rights valid in the originating universe attach to every derivative embodiment, representation, clone, timeline and fictional manifestation of the protected identity" — and then, needing facts and defendants, sends in two hired specialists. They tour the clone territories. A war breaks out among clone factions over the correct wording of one song. The corporation picks a weak defendant for a test case. Both investigators discover, separately and by different roads, that the case is rotten. It goes to trial. The corporation loses its universal claim, keeps a narrow commercial one, and issues a press release calling the defeat a triumph.

### What works

The pairing of the two investigators is well made because the two jobs do not overlap at any point. Duncan Banelure tests paper: he is a copywriter and detective who "understands how authority is manufactured through language, titles, institutional proximity, branding and confident prose," and his real talent is "detecting credential laundering." Adrian Vale tests bodies: he is a performer and movement specialist recruited "to authenticate performers and clones through voice, gesture, timing, physical memory and stage presence." One man asks whether the documents are real. The other asks whether the singing is alive. They reach the same verdict from opposite directions, and neither could have reached the other's half. That is a genuinely efficient piece of construction, and it means the story has two independent engines rather than one engine and a passenger.

> Duncan is investigating counterfeits on behalf of the largest counterfeit authority in existence.

That is the whole idea in one sentence, and it is funny before it is clever, which is the right order. Adrian's equivalent is:

> A certificate can verify the costume. It cannot prove that the song has entered the body.

"The first corporate response is disbelief. The second is valuation" is exactly how a rights-holder would meet the discovery of a parallel universe, and the joke is made by the word order rather than by any elaboration. Similarly, the ending refuses to let the good guys win cleanly, twice over. The corporation loses the big claim and keeps a small one, and then "immediately issues a statement celebrating the ruling as a historic validation of its commitment to responsible stewardship" — at which point "Duncan reads the release and nearly walks back into court." And the clone factions do not reconcile. Their dispute merely changes shape: "no longer over who has the authority to eliminate all other versions" but "an argument over how variants are carried, attributed and performed." The proposal has understood that destroying a corporation's authority does not produce peace, and it says so directly: "The problem is not simply false ownership. It is the desire for one final authorised version."

It is also honest about its own worst risk. Of the pilgrimage material it says: "The narrative should not reduce the Kumbh Mela to exotic scenery." Naming a danger is not the same as avoiding it, but the awareness is real and it is stated before anyone has to point it out.

### Where it does not yet hold together

**The reveal depends on a power nobody has.** Duncan's discovery is that the corporation's chain of ownership is a closed loop in time: "Some precedents cite cases that have not yet occurred. Some agreements appear to have been executed in timelines where the alleged signatories never existed." The proposal's summary of this is lovely — "The corporation has laundered authority through time" — but it requires that somebody, somewhere, can send documents backwards. The proposal does not say who can do that, or how, or what it costs. It is the load the whole story rests on, and it is resting on air. Worse, it gives the corporation a second superpower on top of transdimensional reach, which makes it harder to laugh at rather than easier. This price need not be paid at all — see *Where the corporation stands*, below.

**Act Two is a tour with nothing at risk in it.** The three destinations are the strongest images in the document — a Parkes exemption founded on "an ancient municipal failure to complete the paperwork," a former Burning Man where "participants become Elvis for a night, a season or a lifetime," a pilgrimage where the songs "accompany vows, journeys, bathing rites, processions, mourning, initiation and reconciliation." But nothing in the act threatens anybody. Duncan's "first impossible case" — two clones with equally good genetic and documentary claims singing different words — is a superb scene, and it is presented as a specimen collected on a field trip rather than as a problem that costs either clone anything. The act ends on a realisation ("Duncan realises ABG is not mapping a market. It is mapping a civilisation") rather than on a consequence.

**The other side of the case has no people in it.** The proposal supplies six interpretive lineages — Memphis Continuists, Parkes Variants, Mela Elvises, First-Person Kings, Folk Elvises, Certified Continuities — and then admits that "these names remain provisional." That is fine. What is not fine is that the entire five acts contain no named individual on the clone side. The person whose trial the story turns on is described only as "a vulnerable rogue clone whose performance has enormous popular force but weak documentary provenance." He never speaks. He has no scene before he is a defendant. A story about whether living singers are property cannot leave the singers as a taxonomy, and this is the largest hole in the document as it stands.

**The guide is asked to do too many jobs.** The native figure who spots the visitors is meant to be "guide, irritant and unreliable interpreter," to know the danger before Duncan does, and to break the fictional frame at the climax. Those requirements fight each other. A figure who is unreliable cannot also be the one who correctly explains the theme early; a figure who dissolves the fiction at the crucial moment is doing the author's arguing out loud. And she has no name.

**The trial risks becoming an essay.** Look at what each side is handed in Act Five. The corporation gets five numbered propositions. The clone factions get nine one-word claims — "genetic succession; personal identity; performance lineage; oral transmission; pilgrimage law; public cultural inheritance; religious freedom; historical possession; temporal priority." Set out like that, it reads as a debate transcript rather than a scene. But the answer is already inside the same act, in Adrian's evidence: rather than delivering an expert opinion he "asks rival clones to perform their variants before the court and gathered pilgrims" and then traces where each version came from, "what body carried it, what community altered it and what experience gave it meaning." That is the argument made as action, and it makes every numbered proposition unnecessary.

**Two endings cancel each other.** The proposal wants the guide figure to "break or expose the fictional frame at the crucial moment, showing that ABG itself exists inside a story from the perspective of another universe," so that its "claim to stand outside fiction as the 'real' owner collapses." It also wants the court to reason its way to a real distinction between stewardship, personhood and ownership. These cannot both be the ending. If the plaintiff turns out to be fictional, the careful legal reasoning is beside the point; if the legal reasoning matters, the frame-break is a cheat that arrives just as the argument gets hard. One of them has to go — and there is a way of choosing that costs nothing, below.

**It knows it is missing its own front door.** On how the visitors arrive, the proposal says: "The means of entry should emerge from Mark's canon rather than being imported from Soundchaser." That is exactly the right instinct — and it means the treatment does not currently have a first act, only a request for one.

### Where the corporation stands — the change that resolves the most

The treatment puts the corporation in our world and calls that "the originating world." ABG discovers "that this fictional universe exists," and in Act Five its "claim to stand outside fiction as the 'real' owner collapses." Most of what is difficult above descends from that single decision, and there is an alternative the treatment does not consider: that Authentic Brands Group is not in our world at all, but in a universe next door to the novel's — one member of a family of related universes, of which the novel's world is one and ours is another.

The novel is already built for this. Its narrator routinely places our world among the neighbours rather than above them: a tune "in less mutant universes… would be known as Polk Salad Annie," another that "in a straighter universe would have answered to 'That's All Right'." Universes are ranked by mutation, and ours is one of the duller ones. And the drafted Epilogue already runs cross-universe government as municipal comedy — the ascended child files "his plans for a new Creation with the town planners at Multiversal Council Chambers" and is told "Sure! There's always room for one more." A rights action brought before a body of that kind needs no new machinery and no change of tone.

Six things follow, and between them they are most of the repairs the treatment needs.

The frame-break becomes unnecessary, which removes the competing ending. A corporation from the universe next door never claimed to stand outside fiction, so nothing has to collapse, and the court's reasoning can carry the ending on its own. The treatment's best line survives and improves: "You two aren't from the wrong time. You're from the wrong copyright" now means a different jurisdiction rather than a different order of being — a joke about legal geography, which is the book's kind of joke.

The forged chain of title stops needing a time machine. Every irregularity Duncan finds has a cross-universe explanation instead of a backwards-in-time one. A precedent citing a case that has not yet occurred is a precedent from a universe where it already has. An agreement signed by someone who never existed was signed by the version of him who exists next door. The loop stays closed — ABG holds rights here because a court there recognised them, and that court recognised them because ABG had already exercised them somewhere else — but it now runs through jurisdictions rather than through time. That is a better crime as well as a cheaper one: forum shopping is mundane, grubby and entirely real, where time travel is a superpower.

Ulyssia acquires a reason to be the target. The treatment wants ABG to "win narrowly" against a weak defendant and then "use that precedent to regulate every Elvis body." If the corporation is surveying a family of Elvis-universes, the novel's world is the obvious place to file: it is the one where clones already have no legal standing at all, where the government maintains a Director of Elvi Rights whose only measure of success is "the containment of the rights she was sworn to uphold." The bleakest joke in the novel becomes the corporation's reason for coming, which is a far stronger opening than a reconnaissance mission.

The tour becomes a comparison rather than a catalogue. Parkes, the former Burning Man and the Mela need not be crammed into the novel's geography; they can be other universes' Elvis histories — the ones where the clones were sanctified instead of ghettoised. The imagery survives whole, the contradiction with the novel's ghetto disappears, and Act Two gains the thing it currently lacks: every stop tells Duncan and Adrian what Ulyssia was denied.

The dates stop breaking the novel's rule, because they describe somewhere else. "In 2260" and "the late twenty-fourth century" become statements about a neighbouring universe's calendar rather than a year on this world's page.

And the naming problem changes shape entirely — see Part Five.

## Part Two: what the novel has actually written

Five things in the finished prose bear directly on this proposal, and all five are more useful to it than the proposal seems to know.

### There is already an argument about the correct lyrics, and it is already (comically) violent

This is a running feature of the drafted book. The Elvi "agree on the music, never the lyrics." At the novel's big set-piece festival, a day-long riot builds out of nothing else:

> The punch-ups continued. Who could say who forgot what lyrics and who chose to chance their own? Every deviation emptied the side-stages; festival goers climbed up and fought with bouncers and musicians alike. The crowd loved the double act of concert and fight club… The brouhaha spilled into the wider audience after a Tickle Me series Elvine sang "is your arse filled with paint", and almost every Elvis and Elvine clamoured to the front of the crowd and tried to mount the stage.

Chanta becomes the Elvi's Great Teacher, because he writes lyrics (e.g. "Evolve Me Tender" and "Religious Minds") that somehow all Elvi agree on, while Sarah becomes the Elvi's "Holy Momma" after The Sages of the Apocalypse engineer Sarah's pregnancy with a clone of Clinton Richard Dawkins (The One After the One).

### There is already an authenticity police, and it is the clones themselves

Early in the book, in a Ghetto bar:

> On stage, an Elvis impersonator was singing a tune that more wholesome universes would recognise as A Little Less Conversation, provoking boos and catcalls among the genuine Elvi. Security ushered the would-be Elvis off stage and out the alley door.
>
> "Sorry about that," Lonnie said… "Tourist venturing too far into the Ghetto. I don't know why they try. Don't they know it's cultural appropriation?"

Two things are worth noticing. The judgement is made by ear, by an audience, in a bar, and enforced by a bouncer — no certificate anywhere. And the word the proposal builds its title on is already in the book's mouth, meaning the opposite of a licence: a few pages earlier Tony offers to take Sarah "somewhere authentic, in the heart of the Ghetto."

### A corporation's warranty is already being used as a legal weapon against living clones

This is the closest thing in the novel to the proposal's whole premise, and it is already drafted. In a government cabinet meeting, the Director of Elvi Rights produces a warranty card from the long-dead Grayling Corporation and reads it aloud as though it were live law:

> "This Warranty is immediately null and void, and the ELVIS shall be immediately reclaimable by GRAYLING on commission of any of the following violations: Feeding the ELVIS after midnight; Feeding the ELVIS bacon fat and coffee grounds; allowing the ELVIS to inhale or choke on rhinestones; Exposing the ELVIS—"

She lands on the clause she wants — the one forbidding "religion or religious memes or icons including but not limited to religious texts, psalms, hymns, dashboard figurines, tacky paintings of a blue-eyed Jesus, or… gospel music of any kind" — and slaps the table: "It means this Holy Momma, in whose name Truth was attacked, has voided the warranty on every ELVIS in the attack."

The joke works because the company is dead. Later, Tony reads the same document on his own tablet and says so out loud: "Good luck with that, Grayling you dead taxwit." Elsewhere the point is put — "Grayling Corp is long gone. No one to pay out on it." So the novel has already staged the manoeuvre the proposal wants to build a novel around: paperwork from a vanished corporation, retrieved by officials who need a pretext, declaring living people reclaimable. What the novel does not have is a living corporation on the other end of it.

### Owning a name is already the villain's origin story

The government's chief executive, Piotr Dunhilton, built his career on a naming-rights case:

> As a trade delegation leader, Dunhilton had once sued and technically won the right to call Ulyssian spirits "Scotch". Later, when it was revealed the naming asset was not contractually owned by Ulyssia, but by a Ulyssian citizen, one Piotr Dunhilton, his star rose yet higher. He'd been trading on it ever since.

That is a man who litigated a word into his own pocket and has dined out on it since. The proposal's central grievance already has a face in the book.

### Intellectual property law is already argued about on the page

In one drafted scene three women eat sandwiches out of a stasis fridge and work out that the technology preserving the food may be a reality-shaking artefact. Sarah begins to explain the secrecy law protecting it — "It's to protect the Intellectual Property of—" and is cut off:

> "See?" Kathy demanded. "Here we are again, protecting the people who stole the land for farms in the first place. You can't sing the virtues of IP law without acknowledging its darker side."
>
> …"The same laws that protect Intellectual—I won't use the 'P' word again—are equally good at covering up Intellectual Stupidity."

And the novel's narrator makes jokes about copyright fear in his own voice. Of a whistled tune: "In a spooky coincidence that hinted at the true nature of the multiverse, Chanta channelled the lyrics and sang a line, censored here for the author's legal security, of course." Of a loud act at the festival: "that Funeral-Gabber DJ duo that we can't name for legal reasons and because they threatened to play in our streets if we did."

### The relation between this world and ours is already established — as a joke, in the narration

Songs the reader knows are consistently described as belonging to somewhere else: the tune "in less mutant universes… would be known as Polk Salad Annie"; the one "that in a straighter universe would have answered to 'That's All Right'"; the impersonator singing what "more wholesome universes would recognise as A Little Less Conversation." Our world, where the actual songs exist, is on the novel's own map. It is simply a duller universe than this one.

## Part Three: what is recorded but unwritten

The novel's remaining sixth exists as planning documents, some approved decisions, and a quantity of rough machine-generated draft material that the project's own records mark as unusable. The final part is not written.

The ending has been decided in detail and not drafted. The decisions were recorded on 30 July 2026 and are explicit that nothing in them is finished prose. In brief: the novel's last sentence is "And that's when Sarah's waters broke." On that sentence her son (commonly referred to as "Little Clinton") fulfils the Sages' prophecy and ascends. He finds himself in a council of other ascended beings who "behave exactly like small-time local politicians who unfortunately, have the power to decide the fate of universes in a similar way that a town council may approve or condemn municipal and private activity." They inform the ascended being that his own universe was consumed to make him, that his first job is to build a replacement, and — when he objects to being a god at all — that he has no choice, citing ordinances. He solves it by restoring the universe that was destroyed, exactly as it was, without himself in it. The restored world never learns it was restored. The one trace left behind is the question of what happened to Sarah's baby, and the record marks that as deliberately unanswered.

The record is also explicit on a point that matters for this proposal: "The Sages are correct in their prophecy," including that they knew that the current universe would cease to exist.

A second Epilogue already exists in finished prose. In the written Epilogue the ascended child "spares" the old universe rather than replacing it, because the multiversal town planners tell him "Sure! There's always room for one more" — and he builds a second, kinder world alongside. The Epilogue's last line is its punchline: "Yep. The Elvis of this world was Estonian." Where that written material sits relative to the newer decisions is listed in the project's records as an open question.

## Part Four: the same material, described differently

Here is where the two documents pull against each other, and what follows from each difference.

### Scale and shape of the world

The proposal describes "a vast transcontinental civilisation of clones, performers, pilgrims and rival lyric traditions" whose festivals have become "planetary civic infrastructure," spread across "several jurisdictions, artistic traditions and temporal branches." The novel has one island nation in the southern hemisphere, one city, and a clone population confined to a ghetto with no legal standing whatever — the government's Director of Elvi Rights holds a portfolio whose only measure of success is "the containment of the rights she was sworn to uphold." When the Elvi finally hold a festival on rich ground, they get in by permit fraud: the paperwork declares a classical concert, the rock gear is manifested as orchestral equipment, and, as the novel puts it, "The permit said classical. What the city got was an uprising." And even at that festival, "the Elvi were a minority at their own festival." A civilisation and a ghetto are not the same subject. There are three ways to settle it. The side story happens somewhere and sometime else and is a genuinely separate book. Or it uses the ghetto — in which case its corporation is not fighting a civilisation, it is suing an underclass, which is a different and possibly better story. Or the two are both true in different places: the civilisation belongs to a neighbouring universe's Elvis history, the ghetto is this one's, and the gap between them is the point. That last option keeps the treatment's imagery and the novel's world without either having to give ground.

### What the word "clone" means

The proposal treats its Elvises as clones — copies, some of whom "regard themselves as biological heirs." In the novel the living Elvi are not copies at all. The record is firm: they are descendants of the original manufactured units, "born down many generations across the centuries since the originals were decanted," breeding true enough within a product line to keep its face and its engineered defects. Each is named after the character Elvis played in that line's film. So,m while the novel doges this, its Elvi are the great-great-grandchildren of clones, probably with families (though taht never appears on page), and the proposal's dispute between "biological heirs" and others has already been settled by five hundred years of ordinary reproduction.

### Time and dates

The proposal dates itself twice: "the late twenty-fourth century," and the merger of the festival network with the Kumbh Mela "in 2260." The novel has a standing rule against exactly this. Its own working principles say the story-year "is invisible" — the author works in 2345 in an external timeline tool, "but no year ever appears on the page." Any dated sentence about *this* world breaks a rule the main book has kept for five parts. A date about a neighbouring universe does not, because the rule exists to keep this world's calendar off the page rather than to abolish calendars. That reading is the author's to grant, but it is available.

### Parkes, Burning Man, the Kumbh Mela

None of these appears anywhere in the novel or in any of its notes. This is invention rather than extension, which is permitted, but it should be known rather than assumed. It is worth adding that Parkes is the one import with a natural home: the real Parkes Elvis Festival happens in New South Wales, which is where this novel is set, and the novel already has two festivals of its own — an annual pilgrimage called the Vegas Show, held at Graceland in the Ghetto to celebrate "Momma's birthday," and the political festival that closes the drafted material. Parkes could join that family without strain. A merged planetary Mela cannot.

### How anyone crosses over

The novel's discs do not move people between universes. In the drafted scene where the possibility is raised, the oldest Sage says: "Ancient writings suggest that energies of the kind emitted by Gyges Discs in certain configurations may do more than just channel messages—they may be designed to transmit them." The scientist's reply is the question that follows: "So you're suggesting we could use the discs to communicate with other universes?" That is a telephone, not a door. Which suggests a much cheaper and much funnier front door than the proposal has asked for: what arrives from our universe need not be two men. It could be a letter.

### Backwards causation

There is none in the finished novel. Stasis works on time rather than temperature — the fridges hold "micro-pockets of altered time" — and the discs shake time when spun, but nothing and nobody travels into the past. So the proposal's central discovery, that the corporation manufactured its own precedents by executing documents in the past, is the one thing the novel cannot currently supply, and building it would hand a time machine to a satirical corporation.

It does not have to be built, and this is where the neighbouring-universe framing earns its place. What the novel does supply, in finished prose, is a multiverse of related worlds running at different mutations, and machinery pointed at them: "Ancient writings suggest that energies of the kind emitted by Gyges Discs in certain configurations may do more than just channel messages—they may be designed to transmit them." Cross-universe paperwork explains every irregularity that time travel was invented to explain, and it explains them with what is already on the page. The corporation is not cheating causality. It is filing in whichever reality gives it the answer it wants, and citing the result back home. Same reveal, no new physics, and a crime that ordinary corporations actually commit.

### The guide who does not exist

The proposal says: "Mark's existing Gwenpool-type character becomes the first person to recognise that Duncan and Adrian have arrived from another continuity." No such character could be found — not in the manuscript, not in the character records, not in the notes. Nobody in the novel knows they are in a novel. The closest candidate is Alexander Shakti, a television host and self-appointed police lieutenant who narrates real events as though he were producing them. Faced with an armed standoff he turns to a secret policeman and says:

> "Ah, Agent Jones! On reflection, I think the story requires a raising of the stakes… a little more action, shall we say? I wonder if you'd consider being written in at this point. I'd be happy to allow you and your team a… a reasonably free level of improvisational license."

But that is a man who cannot tell a police investigation from a series, not a man who knows he is printed. The frame-breaking in this novel belongs entirely to its narrator, who addresses the reader directly — "Alert readers may have noticed we referred to documentary records of the Dawkins' Ascension, but how could such accounts exist?" — and who is, elsewhere, plainly some sort of historian writing from outside this universe. So the proposal's guide has three possible homes: Alexander, exactly as written, whose delusion would make him a gloriously wrong interpreter of two men who genuinely are from outside the story; the narrator, who already has the job; or a new character, invented and named. What cannot happen is proceeding on the belief that she already exists.

### Where a trial would actually happen

The proposal wants Act Five to be "part court proceeding, part performance, part doctrinal council and part media spectacle." The novel barely has courts — one drafted scene turns on the discovery that a whole enforcement agency "has never brought such a case in the history of Ulyssia" — but it has something better. Its justice system is a television programme called *Candid Criminal*, on which poor people are tried before a rich studio audience using fabricated evidence and scripted confessions, with audience voting and a Wheel of Death, and with genuine ambiguity about whether the executions are real. Alexander hosts it. The proposal has independently designed the format the novel already owns, and the format is in the hands of a man who would treat a transdimensional rights action as the best commission of his career.

### One Elvis or many

This is the deepest disagreement, and it is not about facts but about what each story is for. The proposal's whole argument is against singularity. Adrian "does not select the true Elvis"; he "reveals that every claim to purity has concealed a history of relation," and the closing image is deliberately plural — rival versions sung at once, "not in unison but in layered variants," forming what Adrian hears as a history rather than a resolution. Set against that, the novel's prophecy promises the opposite, and promises it in finished prose. In a mansion parlour packed with clones, the least discreet of the three Sages lists what the coming new universe will bring:

> Chilo kept ranting: "And there will only ever be one Elvis!"
>
> "You idiot, Chilo," Kleo said, glancing at the stuffed room. "Have you looked where you are? Who you're surrounded by?"

And Kleo then explains what the promise is actually for: "What is important is that the new universe gestates the Elvis who The Dawkins loved so much as a boy." The written Epilogue delivers on it — one Elvis, Estonian, well fed, well doctored, unexploited, dying at ninety-something of an accident involving gold chains and a microphone stand, having lived "as happy as he wished." And the recorded ending decisions state that the Sages are correct.

So the proposal argues that the wish for a single authorised Elvis is the real disease, and the novel grants that wish and treats it as mercy. These can be made to work together — a side story that mounts the best possible case for plurality, which the main story then overrules by giving one man the decent life he never had, is a genuinely interesting pair. But it has to be chosen. If it is not chosen, the two works will simply contradict each other about the thing they most care about.

Once the corporation is placed among the neighbouring universes, something sharper than a reconciliation appears. ABG and the Sages want the same thing. The corporation is trying to establish that there is one authorised Elvis and that it owns him; the prophecy promises that "there will only ever be one Elvis," and the written Epilogue delivers him. Set side by side, the villain's commercial ambition and the scripture the Elvi are dying for turn out to be a single wish with two justifications — control in the corporation's mouth, mercy in the Sages'. Nor is the resemblance only thematic: a corporation asserting jurisdiction over every universe where Elvis appears, and an ascendant deciding which universe gets to exist, are two versions of the same act, someone outside settling what happens to everyone inside. That is not a contradiction between the two works. It is the sort of joke the novel already runs on, and it would give the side story a claim on the main book's ending rather than an argument with it.

### Tone

The proposal asks for "genuine stakes." The novel's own standing instruction runs the other way: it is deadpan satire in the Pratchett and Adams line, and its documented recurring failure is "importing melodrama onto satire" — reading comic material at thriller pitch, treating a villain's bluster as a credible threat when the bluster is the joke. Its chief villain is a humourless hardliner whose lack of humour is the thing being mocked, and the record is explicit that letting him curdle into convincing menace "un-writes the joke." A competent, formidable Authentic Brands Group would be the same mistake at corporate scale. The corporation in this book would need to be funny in the way the government is funny: a machine that is dangerous precisely because nobody inside it can see the absurdity. The proposal's better instincts already point this way — disbelief then valuation, the press release at the end — and its Act Four instincts point the other.

## Part Five: the one practical matter

The novel already has a considered position on Elvis rights, worked out and written down. Its own records note that "the Presley rights have moved to Authentic Brands Group; the current holders are ABG EPE IP LLC and Elvis Presley Enterprises, Inc." A draft front-matter page exists whose central clause states that the book "is not authorised, endorsed, licensed, or sponsored by, and has no affiliation with, the Estate of Elvis Presley, Elvis Presley Enterprises, Inc., ABG EPE IP LLC, or any person or entity holding rights in the name, likeness, recordings, or musical compositions of Elvis Presley."

And the strongest argument the project has assembled in its own defence is this: that the book is "commentary on the Elvis industry itself — the real-world practice of turning a dead man into IP, which is exactly what EPE does," on the reasoning that "you cannot satirise the commodification of Elvis without using Elvis."

*Authentic Brands* takes that argument and makes it literal. That cuts both ways, hard.

In substance it strengthens the case: a story explicitly about a corporation claiming ownership of living people is commentary on the phenomenon, not decoration borrowed from it. In practice it worsens almost everything else. It converts a general satire into a named attack on a specific living company, which raises questions about a corporation's own reputation that the existing analysis does not address at all — the existing work is about copyright and about the personal rights of a dead man, not about what you may say about a company that is alive and litigious. It also points a spotlight at the exact place the project's records identify as the real exposure, which is not the pages but the cover and the marketing: a book whose antagonist is the rights-holder cannot be sold without the rights-holder's name being part of the pitch. And it sits oddly beside a disclaimer whose job is to deny any connection with an entity that would now be a character.

None of that is a reason not to write it. It is a reason to decide it deliberately rather than inherit it from a treatment, and to decide it before the corporation gets a name on a page. The safe version is obvious and is the version the novel already uses everywhere else: invent the company, make the resemblance unmistakable, and keep the real name out. The book already does this with a government minister, a supermarket chain, an office-chair brand and a dead cloning corporation. There is no craft reason the Elvis rights-holder needs to be an exception, and one very good practical reason it should not be.

Placing the corporation in a neighbouring universe changes the posture considerably. A company from the universe next door is, by the novel's own reckoning, a mutation of the real one rather than the real one — the narrator has been sorting realities by mutation since Part One, and has already made the joke at his own expense, breaking off a lyric "censored here for the author's legal security, of course." The book can then field an antagonist that is recognisably the Elvis rights-holder while stating in its own voice that this is not that company but its cousin from a duller reality. That is a stronger disclaimer than any front-matter page, and a funnier one, because it is made of the novel's existing comedy instead of bolted onto the copyright verso. It does not make the real name safe to print, and I would still keep it off. But it turns the problem from a liability to be managed into a joke that does the managing.

## Part Six: what is genuinely open — questions for the author

These are places where neither document decides the matter, and where guessing would be worse than asking.

1. **Where does the corporation stand?** This is the question everything else hangs off, and the treatment settles it by assumption rather than by choice. In our world, as written — which forces the frame-break, the time travel, and the collision with the novel's geography. In a universe next door to the novel's — which removes all three, gives ABG a reason to sue in Ulyssia specifically, and lets the pilgrimage material live in a neighbouring history. Or in the novel's own world, which makes it a domestic corporation and a different story again. My reading is that the second option is the strongest available, but it is a decision about what the story *is*, so it is yours.

2. **Does the guide figure exist?** The treatment assumes a self-aware character already in the novel. Nothing in the manuscript or the records shows one. Is there a character you have in mind who never reached the files — and if not, is Alexander the answer, is the narrator the answer, or is someone new required?

3. **Is a story allowed to name a year?** The no-year rule is absolute for this world. Does it also bind a date describing a neighbouring one?

4. **One Elvis or many — which way does this cut?** Is *Authentic Brands* the case for plurality that your ending deliberately overrules, or does it change your mind about the ending, or should it be kept away from the question entirely?

5. **Does anything ever cross over, or only communicate?** The written novel supports messages between universes and nothing more. Do you want to build travel, or would you rather the invasion arrive as correspondence?

6. **Do you want backwards causation in this world at all?** The treatment's central discovery needs it only while ABG stays in our world. Move the corporation next door and cross-universe filing does the same work with machinery already on the page. If you would rather have the time loop anyway, it changes what the discs are, and it is hard to take back.

7. **Do you want the real company named?** See Part Five. My answer is still no — but the neighbouring-universe framing gives you a great deal more room than the treatment's version does, and I would not decide it by default either way.

8. **How does the drafted Epilogue relate to the ending you set down in July?** Your own records list this as unresolved, and it matters here because the Epilogue is where the "only one Elvis" promise is actually kept.

One note on the limits of this assessment: the two visiting characters, and the project they come from, are not part of these materials, and I did not go looking for them elsewhere. Everything said above about Duncan and Adrian comes from this treatment alone. If their own book gives them abilities, histories or a means of travel that would settle any of the above, that is information this assessment does not have.
