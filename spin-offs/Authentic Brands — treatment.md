# AUTHENTIC BRANDS

*A provisional Clones of Elvis side-quest treatment*

## Core premise

In the late twenty-fourth century, Elvis Presley is no longer merely a historical performer, commercial brand or popular icon. His cloned descendants and licensed embodiments have proliferated across several jurisdictions, artistic traditions and temporal branches. Their fiercest disputes concern not genetic legitimacy but the correct lyrics, meaning and performance of the songs.

Over centuries, Elvis festivals have evolved into a transcontinental pilgrimage culture. Parkes became an early sanctuary of mass Elvis embodiment. Burning Man was absorbed into the movement during its first major expansion. In 2260, the decisive transformation occurred when the Elvis festival network merged with the Prayag Maha Kumbh Mela. What began as tribute became pilgrimage, oral tradition, sectarian theology and planetary civic infrastructure.

Then Authentic Brands Group, the corporate owner of commercial Elvis rights in the originating world, discovers that this fictional universe exists.

ABG declares that its intellectual-property portfolio possesses transdimensional jurisdiction. Before mounting the largest rights action in fictional history, it sends investigators across the boundary to map the new world, identify defendants, establish chains of provenance and find a case capable of converting commercial ownership into universal sovereignty.

Among those recruited are Duncan Banelure and Adrian Vale.

ABG believes it has hired two unusually perceptive specialists.

It has actually imported the two people best equipped to expose the fraud at the foundation of its claim.

## Central dramatic question

> Can a corporation own a living song after that song has become embodied by persons, transmitted as oral tradition, sanctified through pilgrimage and altered by time?

Behind that sits a deeper question:

> Who possesses authority over Elvis: the original performer, the legal estate, the cloned bodies, the singers, the pilgrims, the archive, or the song itself?

## The conceptual inversion

The side quest mirrors the identity problem of The Kin.

Clayton’s one-body doctrine protects continuity where one person’s body, memory and records have been violently disrupted. His world asks:

> How can one altered body remain one person?

The clone universe presents the opposite problem:

> How can many bodies claim continuity with one person?

The complication is that the Elvis clones are not mere duplicate commodities. They have separate experiences, loyalties, interpretations, memories and performance lineages. Some regard themselves as biological heirs. Some claim to be extensions of Elvis. Some treat Elvis as a sacred office. Others insist that “Elvis” is a song-bearing role rather than an individual identity.

ABG’s case depends upon collapsing all of these distinctions into one legal category:

> licensed or infringing Elvis property.

## Principal characters

### Duncan Banelure

Copywriter, detective, avatar and Eternal McGuffin. Duncan understands how authority is manufactured through language, titles, institutional proximity, branding and confident prose. He is selected because ABG needs someone capable of distinguishing genuine Elvis provenance from fabricated claims.

Duncan’s actual gift is detecting credential laundering.

His governing question becomes:

> Who authorised Authentic Brands Group to own authenticity?

He initially treats the assignment as an absurd but potentially lucrative legal investigation. Gradually he discovers that ABG’s transdimensional jurisdiction rests upon circular documents, retroactive precedents and rights instruments whose apparent antiquity may have been manufactured through time travel.

Duncan is investigating counterfeits on behalf of the largest counterfeit authority in existence.

### Adrian Vale

Performer, movement specialist, assistant musical director, disguise expert and reader of embodied incongruence. Adrian is recruited to authenticate performers and clones through voice, gesture, timing, physical memory and stage presence.

ABG expects him to determine which bodies perform Elvis correctly.

Instead, Adrian discovers that the most meticulously licensed Elvises often reproduce the surface while carrying none of the song’s life. Some outlaw variants alter lyrics, rhythm or movement yet embody the emotional truth with unmistakable force.

His governing insight:

> A certificate can verify the costume. It cannot prove that the song has entered the body.

### The native meta-character

Mark’s existing Gwenpool-type character becomes the first person to recognise that Duncan and Adrian have arrived from another continuity.

She understands fictional borders, genre conventions and perhaps the commercial architecture surrounding stories. She becomes guide, irritant and unreliable interpreter. She may know that ABG’s claim is dangerous before Duncan does, because a corporation that establishes jurisdiction over one fictional universe could extend its ownership model to every recognisable figure in every story.

She sees Duncan not simply as a visitor but as a narrative precedent.

## The Elvis claimants

Rather than one unified clone community, the Elvises are divided into interpretive lineages.

- **The Memphis Continuists** insist that recordings and archival documents determine correct performance.
- **The Parkes Variants** treat collective performance and local continuity as the living source of legitimacy.
- **The Mela Elvises** understand the songs as pilgrimage forms whose meaning is renewed through recurrence.
- **The First-Person Kings** believe each viable clone possesses direct standing as Elvis.
- **The Folk Elvises** reject licensing, genetic hierarchy and official lyric sheets.
- **The Certified Continuities** are ABG-recognised Elvises whose legal status, careers and perhaps bodies are contractually governed.

These names remain provisional until Mark’s own world establishes its vocabulary.

## Treatment

### Act I: The rights-holder discovers the impossible

Authentic Brands Group receives evidence that Elvis exists in another fictional universe, not as parody or isolated imitation but as a vast transcontinental civilisation of clones, performers, pilgrims and rival lyric traditions.

The first corporate response is disbelief. The second is valuation.

Internal modelling suggests that centuries of unlicensed Elvis activity represent an incalculable loss of royalties. More importantly, if ABG fails to assert its rights immediately, the clone universe could establish a precedent that culturally evolved figures escape proprietary control once they cross fictional or temporal boundaries.

ABG proclaims the Doctrine of Transdimensional Brand Continuity:

> Rights valid in the originating universe attach to every derivative embodiment, representation, clone, timeline and fictional manifestation of the protected identity.

The corporation cannot yet enforce the doctrine. It needs facts, defendants and a favourable jurisdiction.

Duncan and Adrian are recruited as part of a reconnaissance mission. Duncan is presented as an investigator of provenance, false claims and unauthorised authority. Adrian is presented as a specialist in performance authentication.

Neither is told the full scale of ABG’s ambition.

They cross into Mark’s universe through its existing time-travel or transfictional mechanism. The means of entry should emerge from Mark’s canon rather than being imported from Soundchaser. Promethean Technology may make the contact possible from Duncan and Adrian’s side, but Mark’s universe determines the physics of arrival.

Their arrival is detected immediately by the native meta-character:

> “You two aren’t from the wrong time. You’re from the wrong copyright.”

### Act II: Mapping Elvis country

Duncan and Adrian travel through the principal Elvis jurisdictions to compile ABG’s “lay of the land.”

Their first destination may be the future Parkes festival, now one of the oldest recognised sanctuaries of plural Elvis performance. Parkes retains a peculiar legal exemption founded on centuries of continuous cultural practice. Nobody agrees whether the exemption protects tribute, pilgrimage, parody, personhood or an ancient municipal failure to complete the paperwork.

Thousands of Elvises gather there, licensed and unlicensed, biological and theatrical, solemn and ridiculous. Official registrars count bodies. Parkes counts performances.

Duncan encounters his first impossible case: two clones possess equally persuasive genetic, documentary and temporal provenance, but sing different lyrics to the same song. Each accuses the other of corrupting Elvis.

Adrian watches them perform. One reproduces the surviving recording exactly but feels inert. The other sings a disputed lyric passed down through a Parkes lineage and transforms the crowd.

ABG’s categories cannot explain what Adrian sees.

The mission continues through the former Burning Man territory, where Elvis became fused with temporary-city ritual, radical self-creation and spectacular embodiment. Here Elvis identity is deliberately unstable. Participants become Elvis for a night, a season or a lifetime. ABG regards the entire region as an industrial infringement complex.

Finally they encounter the post-2260 transcontinental Mela. Elvis pilgrimage has merged with the logistical, ritual and cosmological scale of the Prayag Maha Kumbh Mela. The songs are no longer merely performed. They accompany vows, journeys, bathing rites, processions, mourning, initiation and reconciliation.

The precise religious and cultural structures must be shaped carefully with Mark and informed by real expertise. The narrative should not reduce the Kumbh Mela to exotic scenery. Its function in the treatment is to mark the point at which Elvis culture becomes too socially and spiritually embedded to be understood through entertainment law alone.

Duncan realises ABG is not mapping a market.

It is mapping a civilisation.

### Act III: The lyric war

A dispute between major clone lineages escalates over the authorised wording and interpretation of a central Elvis song.

The contested lyric may have several versions:

- the archival recording;
- a rehearsal version;
- a lyric allegedly altered by Elvis himself;
- a Parkes oral variant;
- a Mela liturgical version;
- a version introduced from a future timeline;
- an ABG-certified commercial text.

Each version carries different theological, political and commercial implications. Correcting one word could determine which lineage possesses authority to officiate, inherit, perform or govern.

Violence breaks out between clone factions. Not necessarily armies at first. Performances are disrupted. Stages are occupied. Pilgrimage routes are blocked. Rival Elvises accuse one another of heresy and infringement. ABG quietly encourages the conflict because fragmentation makes the communities easier to litigate and license.

The corporation chooses a test case against a vulnerable rogue clone whose performance has enormous popular force but weak documentary provenance.

ABG’s strategy is to win narrowly. Establish that one clone is an unauthorised derivative embodiment. Then use that precedent to regulate every Elvis body.

Duncan is assigned to assemble the evidence.

Adrian is required to testify that the clone’s performance is inauthentic.

Both begin to understand what they are being used to accomplish.

### Act IV: The provenance trap

Duncan discovers irregularities in ABG’s transdimensional rights documents.

Some precedents cite cases that have not yet occurred. Some agreements appear to have been executed in timelines where the alleged signatories never existed. Key definitions of “derivative embodiment” were inserted retroactively. The doctrine’s chain of title may form a closed temporal loop: ABG possesses transdimensional rights because a future court recognised them, while the future court recognised them because ABG had already exercised them.

The corporation has laundered authority through time.

The native meta-character reveals the greater danger. ABG is not merely seeking Elvis royalties. Once the court recognises that ownership follows a fictional figure across universes, every corporate holder of legacy characters can assert jurisdiction over independent stories, dreams, myths, simulations and emergent persons.

The case against the rogue Elvis is a beachhead into fiction itself.

Adrian reaches his parallel discovery. The rogue clone’s supposedly incorrect performance carries embodied traces from several traditions. His gestures are not copied from archival footage but inherited through teachers, lovers, rivals and pilgrimage. The performance is not pure Elvis.

It is the living history of Elvis reception.

Adrian refuses to authenticate the ABG-certified claimant merely because he is technically exact. He argues that fidelity without living transmission is not authenticity. It is replication.

Duncan and Adrian now face a choice. They can expose ABG, but doing so may strengthen the clone factions’ mutually exclusive claims and intensify the lyric war. Destroying corporate authority will not automatically create peace.

The problem is not simply false ownership.

It is the desire for one final authorised version.

### Act V: The trial of the King

The test case becomes a transcontinental event, part court proceeding, part performance, part doctrinal council and part media spectacle.

ABG argues:

- Elvis is a protected identity.
- Clones are derivative embodiments.
- Lyrics and authorised interpretations remain proprietary.
- Time and fictional displacement do not extinguish ownership.
- Without central control, fraud and cultural corruption proliferate.

The clone factions respond with incompatible claims:

- genetic succession;
- personal identity;
- performance lineage;
- oral transmission;
- pilgrimage law;
- public cultural inheritance;
- religious freedom;
- historical possession;
- temporal priority.

Duncan attacks ABG’s standing. He demonstrates that its supposed transdimensional jurisdiction was manufactured through circular precedent, title inflation and temporal credential laundering.

His decisive formulation may be:

> “You acquired the rights to a dead performer. You have presented that purchase as sovereignty over every living relation formed in his name.”

Adrian then performs his own kind of evidence. Rather than delivering a conventional expert judgment, he asks rival clones to perform their variants before the court and gathered pilgrims. He identifies where each version came from, what body carried it, what community altered it and what experience gave it meaning.

He does not select the true Elvis.

He reveals that every claim to purity has concealed a history of relation.

The native meta-character breaks or exposes the fictional frame at the crucial moment, showing that ABG itself exists inside a story from the perspective of another universe. Its claim to stand outside fiction as the “real” owner collapses.

But the court cannot simply declare Elvis free of all obligations. The clones are persons. Communities have duties. Archives matter. Attribution matters. Exploitation remains possible. Duncan argues for a distinction between stewardship, personhood and ownership.

No one may own an Elvis person.

No entity may claim universal control over cultural or devotional transmission.

Commercial exploitation can still be regulated, but it cannot be used to extinguish living practice, interpretive variation or clone autonomy.

The judgment rejects transdimensional corporate sovereignty.

It does not settle the lyrics.

### Resolution

ABG loses its universal claim but retains some narrow commercial rights within negotiated boundaries. The corporation immediately issues a statement celebrating the ruling as a historic validation of its commitment to responsible stewardship.

Duncan reads the release and nearly walks back into court.

The Elvis factions remain divided, but the conflict changes. Their dispute is no longer over who has the authority to eliminate all other versions. It becomes an argument over how variants are carried, attributed and performed.

The rogue clone sings the disputed song again. Other Elvises join, not in unison but in layered variants. The result is messy, comic and unexpectedly moving.

Adrian hears that the versions do not resolve into one correct lyric. They form a contrapuntal history.

Duncan notices an ABG representative quietly recording the performance for a new compilation release.

The final beat opens the door back into the larger continuities. Duncan receives a cease-and-desist notice addressed not to him but to Duncan Banelure™, alleging that his repeated appearances across fictional universes constitute unauthorised exploitation of his own likeness.

He studies it.

“Finally,” he says. “A case with a sympathetic plaintiff.”

## Thematic spine

The side quest explores:

- **Authenticity versus authorisation.** A licence can confer permission but not truth.
- **Identity versus property.** A living clone cannot be reduced to a derivative asset.
- **Archive versus oral tradition.** Recorded evidence preserves a version; lived transmission preserves a relationship.
- **Fidelity versus embodiment.** Exact imitation may be spiritually empty, while variation may carry continuity.
- **Stewardship versus domination.** The right to protect a legacy does not become sovereignty over every future it generates.
- **One body versus many bodies.** Clayton’s doctrine protects continuity from fragmentation. The Elvis universe must protect plurality from forced singularity.
- **The pharmakon of the song.** The song heals collective rupture, but can also carry hierarchy, commercial control, sectarian violence and longing for a single authorised past.

## Tone

The story should behave as a legal-metaphysical caper with genuine stakes.

It needs the absurdity of cosmic intellectual-property enforcement, thousands of Elvises and bureaucracy conducted in rhinestones. But the comedy should open onto serious questions of personhood, colonial authority, cultural transmission, religious practice and corporate ownership.

Duncan supplies noir irritation, rhetorical suspicion and the legal comedy of manufactured authority.

Adrian supplies bodily intelligence, theatrical exactness and the moral distinction between performing a role and inhabiting it.

The native meta-character prevents either visiting figure from assuming interpretive control over Mark’s universe.

The world itself should remain Mark’s. This treatment provides the crossing mechanism and conceptual case, not its final geography, institutions, cast or canon. Those must be revised after we have properly entered Clones of Elvis rather than peering through the sequinned keyhole.
