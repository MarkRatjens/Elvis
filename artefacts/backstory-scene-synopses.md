# Three backstory scenes

*Written 2026-08-08 at the author's request, from [[original-story-integration]],
[[first-draft-mining]] and the wider knowledge base. **Proposals** — scene shapes
offered for approval, not canon and not plan ([[working-principles]] §12).*

Each item is set out the same way, because the risk in all three is the same:
the past arriving as somebody explaining it.

- **What is true** — author-side. Not what goes on the page.
- **The scene** — a present moment with its own want and its own trouble, in
  which none of the characters is trying to inform anybody.
- **How the past surfaces** — what the reader is shown, what a character says,
  what the reader has to put together alone.
- **Never said aloud** — the sentences that would turn the scene into a lecture.

---

## 1. The medicine

### What is true

Sarah is born sickly. Her mother, **Dr Janine Anstey**, works the viral program
at the Pale lab and begins stealing doses when Sarah is about seven. By eleven
Sarah is not merely well, she is something else. In the week before her twelfth
birthday, Hardy's panel sits and the concrete is ordered. Anstey gives her
daughter everything she has left, goes to work, and is sealed in.

### The scene — a form, a needle, a door

**Whose eyes:** Sarah's, eleven, a week short of twelve.
**What she wants:** a signature.

There is a form. In a world where being sick is a career, everything requires
one, and this one needs a parent's signature and a doctor's — a school trip, a
swimming carnival, some ordinary thing every other child is doing. Sarah has
filled in her half in her best handwriting. She has been not-sick for years now.
She has run out of reasons to be excluded and she has come to collect.

Her mother will not sign it, because the second signature is a doctor's, and a
doctor would take blood.

The row is entirely domestic and completely unwinnable. Anstey does not give
reasons, because she has none she can say. Sarah's case is unanswerable and she
makes it well: *I'm not sick. You fixed me. So what was the point?* — and that
is the closest the truth comes to the surface all night. Her mother shuts it
down the way tired parents do, with nothing, and the argument ends because one
of them has work.

Then the needle, which is where the scene turns. Sarah refuses it — she is
nearly twelve, she is not sick, she is finished with all of this, and refusing is
the only thing left she can win.

Her mother does not argue. She does something she has never done: gives Sarah
everything that is left, all of it, at once, quickly, with her coat already on.
No spoon of anything afterwards. Sarah reads it as being punished. The reader
reads it correctly.

Then: *I'll be back for your birthday.* Then the door.

The scene ends on the door. Nothing after it.

### Optional coda — the birthday

Half a page at most. Cake, an aunt, the aunt's opinions on men, and a chair with
nobody in it. **No grief on the page.** Sarah at eleven behaves the way Sarah
behaves at two hundred and twenty-six: she has a piece of cake and says nothing,
and the flat little nothing is the whole effect. If it can't be that short, cut
it and end on the door.

### How the past surfaces

**Where it sits:** inside Sarah's confession to Chanta, but not in her mouth.
She gives him three flat sentences — the mother, the lab, the vials — and while
he is failing to understand them the book goes there and plays the scene. When
we come back she has already moved on to something else and he has not managed
to ask a question. Her under-explaining is exactly in character, and it means the
scene carries the weight instead of her dialogue.

(The alternative is a chapter of its own placed late. It costs the join to the
confession and gains nothing; I'd take the first.)

**What the scene shows:** a woman refusing to sign a form. A child arguing about
a swimming carnival. An injection. A coat. A door.

**What the reader has to assemble:** everything. That the vials were stolen;
that the hurry was the contractors; that the mother knew she was not coming back;
that the woman "entombed alive" in Gerry's list two centuries later is the one
who put her coat on. None of it is stated at the time and none of it is stated
afterwards.

### Never said aloud

- The words *virus*, *HIBReLabs*, *Hardy*, *lab*, *stolen*, *invincible*.
  Anstey never names what is in the vial, to Sarah or to herself.
- Any version of *this is why Sarah has never been ill.*
- Sarah, at any age, understanding what happened. At eleven she thinks her
  mother is angry with her. At two hundred and twenty-six she has worked it all
  out and mentions none of it.
- Anything that makes Anstey warm. She is stealing a cure for her daughter and
  running a trial on her, and both are true at once. The scene should let the
  reader notice that she takes measurements.

### Canon touches this needs

- [[characters/sarah]] records **one injection at age seven**; this makes it a
  course from about seven to just before twelve. The existing *"never been sick
  since I was seven or eight"* still anchors it — that is when the doses start
  working, not when they stop.
- [[world-rules/biology]]'s dose gradient says "pristine childhood
  **injection**" (singular). Same change.
- Dr Janine Anstey has no character file and needs one.

## 2. A Big Crush

The trap here is the one the project synopsis has already written into Chapter 9
— *"Bit of back story of how Chanta knows Shakti."* A bit of back story is what
this must not be. Play it as chapters, in their own present, with no framing.

### What is true

Six years before Part 1, at a mass-casualty clean-up, a cleaner and a brand-new
police officer are sick behind the same wall. Later the officer needs an
investigation conducted for him, and the cleaner names a price that is not
money. The arrangement has been running ever since. Only one of the two men
thinks it is a friendship.

### Scene A — behind the wall

**Whose eyes:** Chanta's.
**What he wants:** to get through the shift and get paid.

A crowd crushed standing, held upright by rigor mortis, welded into each other
too solidly to prise apart. Hours of trying it respectfully. Then somebody signs
off on a front-end loader and the job stops being recovery and becomes
shovelling.

Chanta ducks behind a wall to be sick and finds the spot taken: a police officer
on his knees, in a uniform that has never been worn before, wearing a rank that
does not exist. He is at the crush because his mother arranged for him to be
photographed at one.

The sandwich: *"Is that real beef?" — "You can have it. I don't think I'll need
it."*

**The turn:** Alexander recovers. And the instant he can stand, the patter comes
back on — the introduction, the rank, the card, the *my good fellow* — and he is
the man he will be for the rest of the book, thirty seconds after being a boy
crying into the grass. Chanta watches the mask go back up and says nothing.

That is what actually happens between them, and it is not fondness. Chanta is
the only person alive who has seen Alexander with it off. Alexander knows it.
Deciding they are friends is cheaper for him than being known by someone, and he
decides it on the spot, and it never occurs to him that it was not mutual.

### Scene B — the price

**Whose eyes:** Chanta's again, at a homicide scene some weeks later.
**What Alexander wants:** to not be found out.

He has been handed an investigation he cannot begin to conduct, with a camera
crew due, and the only person at his crime scenes who does not want him sacked
is the man mopping it. He rings the cleaner.

Chanta walks him through it standing over the body — what the blood says, what
the room says, which of his own officers is lying to him — and Alexander closes
his first case.

**The turn:** afterwards Alexander offers money, generously, in front of people.
Chanta refuses money and asks for the other thing instead: *One day I'll ask you
for something, and you'll do it. No questions, no bill.* Alexander agrees at
once and delightedly. He has just acquired a friend at no cost, and he never
thinks about it again.

### Scene C — the glasses (optional, and the funniest)

Alexander walks a homicide scene wearing camera glasses and an earpiece while
Chanta feeds him every line from a van outside: the salient question, the
judicious allocation of resources, the easy joke with the forensics team. His
own department is astonished at his sudden competence. He says "fuck" for the
first time in his life, out loud, over a corpse, because Chanta told him to.

Keep it a scene, not a summary. It shows the arrangement running, which is worth
more than any sentence describing it.

### How the past surfaces

**Where it sits:** as chapters, at Chapter 9, with **no framing at all** — no
*Chanta remembered*, no *six years earlier*. The time is clear from the inside:
the uniform is new, nobody has heard of Alexander Shakti, and the Stickett Inn
is still standing. The present-day crush gets one line to open the door and does
not explain itself.

**What the reader has to assemble:** that this is the arrangement behind
Alexander ringing him personally at four in the morning for a suicide clean the
night Travis Teh dies; behind *"I contract his cleaning services, that is all"*;
behind the association he cannot make public — which is not the drinking, it is
that his career is a cleaner in an earpiece. And that Chanta is holding a
promise he has never called in.

**What it makes of a line already on the page:** *"I'm glad you got Chanta off
the hook. He's really a very good friend."* Once the reader has seen behind the
wall, that stops being a performance. He means it. That is worse.

### Never said aloud

- *They had not become friends that night, but they had become allies.* The
  first draft narrates its own scene like this; it is the coarse-narrator habit
  the knowledge base flags, and it hands the reader the conclusion they should
  be reaching themselves.
- Any sentence connecting the crush to either man's claustrophobia. Sarah's
  *"It's just as claustrophobic"* already on the page does that job from six
  years away, and does it better for never being explained.
- Chanta thinking about the promise afterwards, ever, until he uses it.
- Anything that makes Alexander decent. He is not repaying a kindness, he is
  enjoying a friendship he invented. Chanta remembers a receipt; Alexander
  remembers a mate; neither has ever mentioned it to the other, and the gap
  between the two memories is the joke.

### What it hands Part 6

A card that is not a punch. When Chanta finally calls it in, the ask should be
small, entirely within Alexander's power, and cost him the only thing he has —
the show.

## 3. How Hardy killed the cure — a chapter

**Whose eyes:** Jamison Hardy's, start to finish, with the narrator taking the
last word. Two centuries before Part 1. He comes off the referenced-only list
for this chapter and goes straight back onto it.

**The register:** office admin. Not a thriller, not a horror. Hardy is
courteous, tidy, faintly sad and entirely convinced he is doing something
noble, and he is never once shown enjoying any of it. Nobody raises their voice
in the whole chapter. The cruelty is in the paperwork.

**What changes:** it opens with a cure for every human disease in existence and
Hardy the only man alive who has been told. It closes with the cure under fifty
metres of rock along with the people who made it, and Hardy certain he is a
hero — and right that nobody will ever know.

### The movements

**1. Good news, badly received.**
Hardy is in a meeting he is enjoying — the landmarks campaign, the protection of
culturally significant harbour frontage, money being spent on nothing at all.
The lab's result comes up the line and is presented to the room as the finest
thing the company has ever done. Everybody is delighted. Hardy is the only one
who goes quiet, and the reason he goes quiet is that he is the only person in
the room who owns anything. He thanks the presenter warmly, twice, and asks that
the file not be copied.

**2. He does not consult a scientist.**
He runs through who he could ask, and every one of them is wrong. Scientists
will say yes. Doctors will say yes. Ministers leak. The board would want a
briefing, and a briefing is a document with names on it. What he wants is an
answer he can hold up, produced by people with no stake and no standing.

So he takes the finance department, and from the finance department he takes the
juniors: a dozen accountants, economists and finance advisers who model things
for a living and have never once been asked a question that mattered. They are
told they have been selected. Several assume it is a promotion. One brings a
folder of ideas he has been waiting four years to show somebody senior.

**3. Down.**
The file cannot leave the site, so the modellers go to the file. Fifty metres,
under the Pale. The chapter's only atmosphere is here and it should be brisk:
the lift, the pressure doors, a corridor, a man in a good suit who does not know
his own way and has to stop and read a sign. The juniors are impressed with
themselves. Somebody takes a photograph and is asked not to.

**4. The brief.**
A conference room. Bad light, a whiteboard, a jug of water. Hardy tells them he
does not know any of their names and has declined to be told them: *"You have
been brought here precisely because I don't know you."*

One question — what happens to the economy if disease ends. Seven hours. He does
not say why seven. He is asked, and says "operational".

Then the questions from the floor, every one of them professional and every one
beside the point: is this a memo or a deck; is there a template; are we assuming
current tax settings; is the audience the board or the regulator; what happens if
we need longer. Somebody asks about lunch. Hardy has thought harder about the
catering order than about anything else that morning, and it is a very good one,
and he is pleased to be able to say so.

**5. Seven hours upstairs — and Hermann comes up.**
What Hardy does while they work: he has the drawings for the dome open on the
table, and he is costing it. Cubic metres. He is extremely good at this. The
contractors are on site because he sent them before he asked the question, and
the reader gets to notice that on their own.

**Then his friend comes up to see him.** Hermann is delighted — Jamison, all
this way, and obviously about the result. He has brought a printout. He wants to
talk about scale-up, about a bigger trial, about whether they can afford to
simply give it away, and whether they can afford not to. He talks about
children. He mentions, in passing, the colleague running the field work, the one
with the sick daughter, who has been all but living down here.

Hardy listens. He tells him it is magnificent, which he believes. He tells him
to keep going. He shakes his hand. He does not say one untrue thing, and he lets
him go back down.

**6. The report.**
It arrives on time, because juniors deliver on time. It is competent. Executive
summary, assumptions page, three scenarios, a risk register, an appendix nobody
will ever read. The recommendation is one sentence: *the miracle drug must be
destroyed, all research must be destroyed, and all related research staff must
be eliminated.*

Nobody in that room wrote it in anger. They arrived at it, checked it, and are
quietly pleased with it, the way you are pleased with a piece of work that came
out clean.

**7. The one who works it out.**
At the door, bag over the shoulder, halfway through asking about the car home,
one of them does the arithmetic on *all related research staff* and stops
walking.

And is embarrassed. And the others are mildly annoyed at him for being dramatic
at the end of a long day.

That is the last thing that happens in that room in this book.

**8. Hardy reads it and is relieved.**
Here is the mechanism of his heroism, put plainly and never commented on: he
asked a question he already knew the answer to, and the answer has come back
from outside himself, in writing, from people whose names he does not know. So
it is not an opinion. It is a finding. He is complying with it.

He carries that for two hundred years and it never cracks once.

**9. Procurement.**
The recommendation says destroy, so destroy gets costed. Incinerating a facility
at that depth, against sealing it. The concrete comes in cheaper, and — this is
the line the rest of the novel hangs off — **it produces a receipt.** A
destroyed thing has to be certified destroyed by somebody who went down and
looked. A sealed thing is certified by an invoice.

The order goes out as one line in a board paper and is executed the same week
across a dozen installations by people who never met him and never asked. Nobody
is evacuated. The chapter does not describe the sealing and does not go back
down.

**And that is how both viruses survive** — intact, catalogued, dry, fifty metres
down, waiting two centuries for a man with a drill and a deadline. Hardy
destroys the cure by preserving it perfectly, and neither he nor anybody else
ever notices.

**10. The car, and the narrator.**
Hardy going up, deciding what he is. He has saved the entire world and no one
will ever know, which he understands to be the curse of the true hero. He is
sentimental about it. He may well be a little tearful, and the chapter should
let him be, because that is the joke and it does not need pointing at.

Then the narrator takes the last word — briefly, in the voice that says *our
streets* and *in less decrepit universes*. Nobody ever found out. Nobody ever
will. He was right.

### What the chapter must not do

- **No villain speech.** Hardy never explains the economics of illness to
  anybody. Dunhilton recites that finding as his own common sense two centuries
  later, having never heard of the room it came out of, and the echo is the
  payment.
- **Nobody dies on the page.** No glass dome, no cook-up, no murdered security
  manager — first-draft scaffolding, and all of it weaker than the invoice.
- **Nobody pleads, struggles, or bangs on a door.** The last thing in the room
  is a man being embarrassed about his lift home.
- **Hardy is never cruel and never doubts.** No flicker of conscience he acts
  on, no hesitation the reader can bank. Courtesy the whole way through.
- **No character in the rest of the book ever learns any of this.** Gerry's
  *"Apparently not"* stays a shrug forever. Sarah knows what happened to her
  mother and nothing whatever about that conference room.
- **No date, no caption.** No year on the page and no *two hundred years
  later* — the narrator's closing sentence carries the time by itself.

### Placement

Late, and well away from Gerry's excavation. Next to the dig the reader waits
for somebody to find out, and nobody does. Near the ending it sits alongside a
restored universe that carries no memory of having been remade: an enormous
thing that decided everything, that nobody knew and nobody ever will — the small
rehearsal of the large joke.

### What it costs

Hardy stops being referenced-only. He gets a face, a manner and a voice, and
[[characters/jamison-hardy]] becomes a real character file rather than a set of
anchor facts. Worth naming up front, because it is not recoverable afterwards.

## The join between 1 and 3

Anstey gives her daughter everything she has left on the afternoon the panel is
locked in, and she is in a hurry because she can hear the mixers at the far end
of the level. In this chapter she is one clause in Hermann's small talk — the
colleague with the sick daughter, mentioned in passing by a man who has come
upstairs to be congratulated.

Neither scene mentions the other. The reader who gets to both has the whole
thing, and is never told they have it.
