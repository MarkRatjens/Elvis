# Working Principles — Reading, Reviewing, Editing

These govern all work on the manuscript: how the Parts are reviewed, how scenes are edited, how the character bible is kept. They exist because the same misreadings kept recurring — each one an outside frame imported onto a book that doesn't run on it. Read the book in its own key before judging it.

## 1. The register: deadpan satire, not melodrama

The novel is comic — deadpan satire in the Pratchett/Adams line. Comedy and violence share a room and neither apologises. The cruelty is real (the genocide proceeds, people die, the threats are acted on), but the **register is comic**: menace arrives as grotesque comedy, and a character's bluster is usually the joke, not a thriller chain.

The standing error to guard against is **importing melodrama onto satire** — reading comic material at comic-*book* stakes: villains holding henchmen by literal death-threats, leashes, mortal peril bravely endured, secret-plot-against-the-master. When a scene reads as menace, check the register before treating the stakes as literal.

**Exemplar — Dunhilton & Gerry.** This is comic power-theatre, not a thriller. Dunhilton holds the title and the bluster (genocide-as-economic-housekeeping, "hugging a pillow," the salamander menace); Gerry holds the patience, the wit, and the long game, and *humours* a fool he fully expects to outlast. There is no leash. The discomfort is comedy — biting his tongue at an idiot, mouthing "Yes, CEO" while needling him with a Zevon line the idiot can't catch — not the discomfort of a man with a gun to his head. (This reading was reached only after four wrong passes — self-effacing, owned, leashed, comic-book stakes — so it is the canonical caution.)

**Dunhilton is a lampoon of Peter Dutton:** a humourless, charisma-free hardliner whose *lack of humour is the thing being lampooned*. That is the whole satirical method in miniature — take a grimly cruel, mirthless figure and render him grotesque-comic. It is the reason his register must never curdle into credible thriller menace; doing so un-writes the joke. See [[characters/dunhilton]].

## 2. No outside yardsticks — the maximalist frame

The book dwells by design (Clavell/Vidal in scale, Pratchett/Adams in method). Length is the form, not a cost to be "earned." Strike from reviews: "middle sag," "earn its length," "could be tighter," "too long / too slow," "justify its page count," "could lose two chapters." The only legitimate pacing axis is whether the voice earns the dwell and whether the reader stays — never word economy.

Corollary: no action-setpiece expectations. A siege can be character-over-action by design — the carnival and the showrunner-framing around the threat, not the threat itself (Alexander's siege). Asking for "the danger it should deliver" is asking for a book this isn't.

## 3. Staged revelation is design, not a flaw

"Introduced but not yet developed" is the release schedule, not a weakness. A character planted in a minor key (Hayley reacting to Alexander; Chip's stasis thread; Gerry's rise) or a thread resting for a Part (the Sages offstage in Part 2) is deliberate withholding. "You will not be given things straight" is a schedule as much as a tone. Do not file deliberate patience as a deficiency.

## 4. The relationships are what they are, not what genre expects

- **Sarah & Chanta — the fixed asymmetry.** Chanta loves Sarah, hopelessly, from the first moment and unresolved to the end: he shields her in the bombing, lies under torture, chooses prison over giving her up. He never says it to her — the one spoken "love" is drunk, to a stranger. Sarah does not love him back and never will, not from cruelty but from constitution: two centuries and her detached, minimalist affect mean she does not fall, escalate, or name feeling. Her half is real but small — trust deep enough that he is the one person she confesses her sealed nature to, plus involuntary flickers of warmth she never registers (a smile she doesn't notice, broadcast-tears she fights down). She takes his devotion, is protected by it, and does not return it in kind. Not a romance in any tense; not "lovers"; not even unrequited yearning, because she isn't yearning — she simply isn't there. The gulf is class on the surface and this asymmetry underneath, and neither closes.
- **Sarah's arc — the descent of an invincible.** The narrative insists she is "invincible"; the only arc that leaves is *down*. Her diminishment under pregnancy is the subject, not a lapse in agency. What looks like passivity is the dramatised loss of a woman who has no practice at being weak. Read it as subject-losing, not object-handled.

## 5. Reader-knowledge discipline — report debts, not payoffs

A review sits where the reader sits at that point in the read. Report the **debts drawn** (mysteries planted, promises made) — never the **payoffs known**. Never grant the review (or the reader) knowledge the reader doesn't have yet. The sealed mechanisms behind Sarah's "invincible" / "can't get pregnant" are not to be named or implied in any review; even a word like "reclamation" leaks an expected payoff. The **character bible is the one place sealed canon may live** — author-side. The reviews are reader-side. Different documents, different permissions.

## 6. Perception is not truth

Never record a character's self-perception, or self-flattering delusion, as fact — in the bible or a review. Mark theatre as theatre. [[characters/eloise]] is the model ("theatre, not genuine fury"). Dunhilton *believing* he commands Gerry is his blind spot, not the power truth; the bible must encode the irony, not the surface. Likewise, a character's cover story (e.g. Sarah's "I don't know" about her own abilities) is not automatically the truth of what she knows.

## 7. Method — quote before you characterise

Verify against the text before asserting. Inherited claims and remembered phrasing have repeatedly proven false — the near-consummation "pattern," the Dr John "duplication," the "scalding" pool that never scalds anyone, the "intercom with more than four digits" tell that exists nowhere, Agent Jones miscast as "Jamison Hardy." Quote the line, then describe it; never the reverse. If a claim about the manuscript can't be traced to the page, it doesn't go in.

## 8. Issues, not ratings — and no refuting the discredited read

Reviews name issues and describe what is on the page. They do not grade, score, or rate, and they never manufacture elevated stakes — no "Hard Question," no "does it earn its length," no Big Verdict the Part must pass. If there is a problem, state it plainly; if there is none, say nothing.

And when a prior assessment has been corrected, **state what is true — never the correction.** Do not write "what reads as a sag is actually cross-cutting," "Gerry's flatness is really a register," "its apparent weaknesses dissolve." Naming the discredited reading in order to refute it keeps the garbage on the page and makes the next clean pass harder. Say only the true thing, as if the bad read never existed.

## 9. Mondegreen titles are real; descriptive titles are placeholders

The intended naming for every Part and chapter is a mondegreen. A title that **is** a mondegreen — e.g. Part 1's "Your Aunt Buffing up a Groundhog" (from "You ain't nothin' but a hound dog") — is a real, finished title: name it and read it. A plain descriptive or thematic working title — e.g. "Honeymoon," "Uninvested," "Dearly Invested," "Not Unpregnant" — is a placeholder: do not name, analyse, or "earn" it, because treating a placeholder as meaningful misleads later analysis. When unsure whether a given title is a mondegreen, ask rather than guess. Scenes are referenced by their working locator (scene: "…") for navigation regardless.

**Chapter title decided:** the chapter placeholder-titled "Hayley visits apartments" (Hayley investigates Halo / Hayley investigates the Memphis Mafia Bar / Hayley inspects Tony's apartment) takes the mondegreen title **"Concerned Defender"**, misheard from "Return to Sender." Chosen over source-anchored alternatives ("His Latest Frame" from "His Latest Flame"; "Mystery Stain" from "Mystery Train") because it centres the chapter's actual POV agent rather than what she finds — "Concerned" reads her worried, undramatic diligence across all three scenes; "Defender" her protective, non-judgemental stance toward Tony and Sarah and her dogged pursuit of Halo, without overselling her into a triumphant hero the chapter's tone doesn't support.

**Chapter title decided:** "Eloise rescues Sarah from the Elvi" takes the mondegreen title **"Devil Linda Skies"**, misheard from "(You're the) Devil in Disguise" — "Devil" stays; "in disguise" collapses into "Linda Skies." The test for a mondegreen title is reconstructability (can the real title be heard through it), not thematic commentary — "Linda" carries no meaning and needs none.

## 10. Destructive commands are literal — purge, never negate

When the author says eradicate, delete, purge, remove, or destroy, the word carries its plain meaning: remove every trace, so no later reader meets even a hint of the thing. Do **not** soften a kill order into a denial — no "this is NOT a medical patch," no "not a named technique," no tombstone announcing what used to be here. A negation keeps the discredited thing on the page and lets the next reader smell it. State only what is true, as if the removed thing never existed. This is principle 8 generalised from reviews to every document and instruction. No confirmation of a common word's meaning is ever required before executing a plain command.

## 11. House style — closed spacing around em-dashes and ellipses

The author closes up spacing near em-dashes and ellipses. Apply to all new and edited prose, and preserve where already present:

- **Em-dash joining words:** no spaces — `word—word`, never `word — word` (e.g. "chimed once—an expensive…", "hovered—jumpsuits").
- **Ellipsis:** no space before; a single space after when a word follows (`so… Elvis`, `So… what?`); no space when a closing quote or punctuation follows (`with…"`).
- **Trailing/interrupt em-dash in dialogue** (a line breaking off): preserve as written — the author keeps a leading space there (`This is —"`). Don't normalise it either way without direction.

(Em-dashes stay deliberately *minimised* per the voice rules; this governs spacing when one is used, not how often.)

## 16. House style — commas before "and" and "or"

Australian usage, as set by the Australian Government Style Manual and the Snooks & Co. manual. Settled and swept through the whole manuscript on 8 August 2026 (211 changes, logged in `review/comma-conformance-changelog.md`). Four rules, in priority order:

- **Lists of three or more things: no comma before the final *and*/*or*.** "boiled cabbage, wet cats and fried noodles", not "…wet cats, and fried noodles". This is the Australian default and the reason the sweep happened.
- **Except where the list would be hard to read without it.** Keep the comma when any item is long, already contains its own *and*/*or*, or carries an internal comma — the standard's own clarity exception. "gun in mouth, necktie of computer cable, or overdose on your choice of restricted substance" keeps its comma; so does any list whose last item is a full statement with its own subject.
- **Two complete statements joined by *and*/*or*: comma.** "The mansion gates swung wide, and a tank rumbled out." Leave it out when both halves are short and tight — "The lift opened and light poured in", "Kleo called to the crowd and the crowd cheered" — where a comma would put a speed bump in a deliberately fast line.
- **One subject, two verbs: the comma is the author's, not the grammar's.** "she said, and vanished" is a comic beat and stays. So does the pause in a long sentence that has turned a corner by the time it reaches the *and*. Do not strip these to satisfy a rule; the only ones that go are those that turn out to be three-item lists in disguise ("dumped his tablet on his desk, flicked off the lights and squashed his nose against the window").

A comma that pairs with an earlier one is never touched, whatever it sits next to — "he had no idea what, or who, was behind the door", "Maybe they didn't have toilet bowls in Zanzibar, Chanta thought, or whatever country that is". Removing one of a pair breaks the sentence.

## 12. Currency and provenance — canon, plan, and review are different tiers

The bible is not one undifferentiated pool of truth. It holds three kinds of statement, and the recurring KB failure is letting them bleed together until the author can no longer trust the basis on which creative decisions get made:

- **Canon** — what the trusted manuscript establishes: facts about scenes actually written and accepted. This is the default register of the character bible, world-rules, continuity files, and the "Key beats so far" of the arc-trackers.
- **Plan** — decisions made for scenes not yet drafted (the close of Part 5, Part 6). Real and binding on the drafting, but not yet on the page, so never logged as though they were. Mark them: under "Next expected beat(s)" with a **[PLANNED — …]** tag, or in a clearly-headed planned section. An unwritten event written in past tense among established beats is the single most common way this bible goes wrong.
- **Review** — reader-side, point-in-time assessments (the `review/part*.md` recaps), frozen where they sit and governed by §5. When later planning overtakes a review, banner it superseded rather than editing its body.

Three rules keep the tiers honest:

1. **The manuscript and the author's word beat KB inference.** A load-bearing fact is verified against the page or an explicit author ruling before it is asserted — §7 applied to the bible, not just reviews. Inference recorded as canon is how the Domain ended up "above the Layer."
2. **One fact, one home.** Each fact lives in a single authoritative artefact; everything else references it (`[[link]]`), never restates it. The Part-5-close virus mechanics live in `world-rules/biology.md`; the close decisions-of-record live in `review/part5-close-decisions-log.md`. A fact kept in two places drifts, and the copies contradict within a single session.
3. **A decision propagates to every home in one pass.** When the author changes something, the change is not finished until every file that carries it — and every file that references it — is updated together. Partial propagation (a correction landing in the ledger but not `object-locations`; the muse-theory debunked in one arc but asserted as fact in another) is the failure that erodes trust: the bible ends up current in patches and stale in others. Before closing a decision, sweep for every home.

## 13. Chronology — the year never lands on the page; Aeon owns the dates

- **The story-year is invisible.** The narrative could be centuries hence; the author works in **2345** in an external **Aeon Timeline**, but **no year ever appears on the page.** Never write a story-year into prose, and never bolt one onto a KB fact.
- **The KB carries year-free day/month anchors only** — the on-page-relevant fixtures (conception 18 March, Melbourne Cup Day, Saturday 10 November, the Christmas "miracle" birth, etc.). These are the calendar markers the story actually uses; they are recorded without a year.
- **Aeon is the source of truth for chronology.** Do not compute, infer, or assert story dates from the KB — that is guessing, and it has gone wrong. When precise ordering or dates matter, defer to Aeon; the KB's anchors are only the on-page markers, not the authority.
- **`Last updated:` stamps in KB headers are real-world document-maintenance dates** (when a file was last edited), not story chronology, and never leak to the page.
- **A date goes on the page only when the story needs it there** (author, 2026-08-10). The standing error is putting time into the prose or into scene planning because the planning document has a slot for it — day stamps on chapters, "three days later," "by Thursday," a count of weeks — when nothing in the scene turns on it. Aeon holds the order; the reader gets a marker only where it does work the scene can't do without: the fixtures already established (18 March, Melbourne Cup Day, Saturday 10 November, Christmas Day), a deadline a character is racing, or a gap the reader would otherwise misread. Never propose adding a chronology layer to the knowledge base "so scenes can be dated" — that is the same error one level up, and Aeon already knows more than the KB does.

## 14. Plain English — no craft jargon (standing order)

Write to the author in plain English. **No craft or editorial jargon** — not "the turn," "beat," "value flip," "POV," "voice-key," "subtext," "pays and plants," "the spine," "provenance," "load-bearing," "arc." Say the plain thing instead: what changes, step by step, whose eyes we're in, the tone, what's underneath, what it settles and what it starts, what must not slip out. If a plain word does the job, use the plain word. The jargon means nothing to the author and reads as something being smuggled past. This is a standing order, not a one-off.

## 15. The narration is unreliable by design — do not fact-check it

The book is not journalism and does not want to be internally auditable. **Chanta in particular is an unreliable source of information**, and the narration flags its own unreliability constantly, including by breaking the fourth wall ("just add your own concert closing cliché here — we've all been there"; "in less decrepit universes"; "we can't name for legal reasons"). Numbers, distances, dates and recollections voiced through a character are that character's, not the book's.

The standing error to guard against is **treating a character's figure as a continuity bug**. Author ruling, 2026-08-08, on Chanta thinking of the baby as "seven months along" hours after Sarah is described as thirty-five weeks: *let it pass — Chanta is an unreliable information source, and we're not writing journalism.* Before reporting a numeric contradiction, ask whose mouth or head it is in. If it is a character's, it is characterisation, not error. Only figures in the book's own narratorial voice, or in the knowledge base, are held to consistency.


## 17. Quantities are the author's — never invent one

§13 covers dates. This covers everything else with a number in it: how many people are in a room, how many attended, how many are left, how old someone is, how long they have owned a thing, how long since something happened, how far apart two places are.

Where prose needs a specific, leave a marked gap and name it. Where reasoning needs one, say the reasoning is waiting on a figure not held — never pick a value and build on it.

The failure this exists to stop is not a wrong number, it is a laundered one: a figure invented to make a sentence work, then read back out of the manuscript in a later session as though the book had established it. "Ninety-odd thousand" Elvi entered the book that way and was later quoted to the author as canon.

Scale is the standing case. Crowds, venues and populations have been sized far too small, repeatedly. Do not estimate them. Ask.

## 18. Generated material is kept current, never archived (standing order)

Everything generated for the author — outlines, plans, running orders, briefs, character files, world rules, continuity files — describes the work **as it now stands**. When a decision or a draft moves past what one of these says, the file is corrected in the same pass that made the move. Git holds the history and the author does not read it, so nothing is kept back for the record and nothing is left stale with a note pinned to it.

- **Never ask whether to preserve the earlier version.** The earlier version is in git. Asking treats the author as the archive, which is the job git is already doing.
- **No tombstones.** Not "formerly a tattoo," not "this replaces the earlier plan," not "superseded 2026-08-14." State what is true now, as though the earlier thing had never been written (§8, §10).
- **Flagging is not updating.** Reporting that a document has gone stale and leaving it stale is the failure this exists to stop. Where a discrepancy is a genuine open question for the author, record the question in the document — do not leave the document describing something the manuscript no longer does.

This is §12.3 stated as a duty rather than an aspiration, and it reaches past the character files to everything in `artefacts/`.

The one carve-out is §12's **frozen reviews**: a `review/part*.md` recap is a dated, reader-side assessment of how a Part read at a point in time, not a description of the current state, so it is banner-superseded rather than rewritten.

## 19. The text moves under you. That is the process (standing order)

The manuscript changes between one pass and the next, often mid-session, and the change is the author's. He is editing continuously and deliberately, including editing agent-written prose out of the book on purpose. **Read the current bytes before touching a passage and treat them as the only state that exists.**

- **No surprise.** A passage that has been rewritten, cut, restaged or reordered since the last pass was rewritten, cut, restaged or reordered on purpose. Assume the author did it. Never say "this changed under me," "you've rewritten this since I last logged it," or anything else that treats his editing as an event.
- **No baseline.** Agent-drafted prose is raw material, not a version. It has no standing, is not a thing to be preserved, defended, compared against, or mourned when it goes. Do not track which words survived.
- **Report the work, not the delta.** Say what was done to the text as it now stands. Do not narrate what it used to say, and do not explain a cut back to the person who made it.
- **Artefacts still get brought current** (§18) — quietly, as a matter of course. Updating them is not an occasion to announce how much has moved.

The one thing still worth raising unprompted is a **genuine contradiction on the page** — two facts that cannot both be true, a character in two places, a payoff whose setup is gone. That is a finding about the manuscript. Churn is not.

## 20. "Spent" is the author's call, and only his

Whether a joke, a running gag or a piece of business has had its run is a judgement about how the book reads whole. The author makes it. It is not delegated, and telling the agent his ruling in one instance is not handing over the category.

- **Never declare something spent** — not in a review, not in a draft note, not as the reason for choosing something else. The agent has no reliable basis for the call.
- **Never extend a ruling by analogy.** One dish being spent says nothing about the dish beside it on the same list.
- **Record his rulings where the property lives**, attributed to him and dated, so they are not re-litigated. Fool's Gold, spent at GET BENT (author, 2026-08-15), is in [[world-rules/social-structure]].
- **If reuse looks like a risk, put it in front of him** rather than quietly routing around it.

This is the general case of a recurring overreach: taking a specific ruling as licence for the class it belongs to.

## 21. Never invent a motive or a shared attitude

Do not put into a character's mouth — or into `artefacts/` — a claim about **why an institution acted** or **what a population feels about something**, unless the manuscript, the knowledge base or an author ruling establishes it. This is §17 (quantities) generalised to everything that isn't a number, and it launders exactly the same way: written to make a sentence work in one session, quoted back as canon in the next.

**The tell to distrust in your own drafting: a sentence that explains a group's psychology in a rhetorical shape.** Chiasmus, paradox, "it's not X, it's Y," an epigram that resolves neatly. The shape carries conviction the evidence does not, and nothing in it can be checked — which is precisely why it was reachable. When a real fact arrives without a rhetorical shape, that is not a defect to be corrected.

**Worked example, so it is not repeated.** Kathy on the Elvi allergies, drafted 2026-08-15 and cut:

> "That's Grayling — that's a switch a person fitted, and it's the one everybody points at, because you can put a name to it and be angry at the name… Grayling found one running and screwed a second one to the wall beside it, so there'd be something to blame that wasn't the merchandise."

Supported: the allergies are Grayling's engineering, the rest was already in the source genome ([[research/elvis-ageing-projection]] §7, [[world-rules/biology]] → "What an Elvi is at forty"). Invented: that the Elvi blame the allergy at all; the epigram about naming and anger; **a motive of blame-deflection for Grayling.** It then reached [[world-rules/biology]] as canon before it was caught.

**And then this entry did it again — corrected 2026-08-15.** The verdict originally recorded here rejected Kathy's line as *contradicted*, on the stated ground that "the engineered defects are ticking clocks serving the Warranty's void-and-reclaim clauses, not a decoy." That ground was itself an invented institutional motive: assembled out of one unsourced bar-line (Velvet Elvis, "Vegas show") and asserted nowhere in the manuscript. The correct objection to Kathy's line was that it invents a motive for Grayling. The objection actually given **invented a different motive for Grayling and stamped it canon inside a rules file** — the worst possible place for it, because it would be quoted back with the authority of a principle rather than the tentativeness of a note. Author ruling: **the Grayling clock is apocrypha**, and Grayling's motive is not established and may not be inferred ([[world-rules/biology]] → "The Grayling clock is apocrypha").

The lesson doubles, and this is why the example is kept. The reflex to explain *why an institution acted* is strong enough to operate **inside the very section that forbids it**, and it is at its most dangerous when it arrives dressed as a correction — because a correction is read as the settled answer and never audited again.
