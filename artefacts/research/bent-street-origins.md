# Bent Street — Origins of the Real One

Research reference for the "Get Bent Street" chapter (scene `7F9C84D7`, drafted 2026-07-23) and
the Occupy Bent Street material generally. Two jobs: (1) real-world texture for humorous
exposition in the narration, (2) a dimensions ruling for the **Bent Street plaza** — the
above-Layer civic frontage at Airborne "ground," the lowest occupied level above the Layer
(see [[../world-rules/geography|geography]], "Bent Street" entry and the two-level CBD rule).

Last laid down: 2026-08-02 | Sources at foot. Load-bearing facts (naming, dates, institutions)
verified across authoritative sources; softer inferences flagged inline. Street dimensions
measured from OpenStreetMap roadway geometry, not from a published figure.

Do not confuse with Bent Street, Paddington or Bent Street, Concord — different streets,
different Bents.

## The joke history already made (read first)

The real Bent Street is a satirist's gift that requires almost no embroidery:

- **It is named after a judge, and it also bends.** Ellis Bent, Deputy Judge-Advocate of New
  South Wales, honoured in Governor Macquarie's street-naming proclamation of **6 October
  1810**. And the street genuinely doglegs — the line kinks on the order of 30–40° where it
  crosses O'Connell Street (measured from the modern street geometry), with a second curve
  added later at Chifley Square. A street named for a lawyer that is also crooked: the one
  recorded instance of truth in advertising in the legal profession.
- **"Get bent"** — the street's name and the slang insult are a ready-made collision, there
  for anyone angry enough to arrive at exactly the right address. (In-book canon, per the
  author's notes: "GET BENT" is spoken among the protesters and propagates by voice — there
  is no fixed sign.)
- **It really was the government's front yard.** For the street's first thirty-five years its
  northern side was the **garden and grounds of First Government House**. The drafted line
  "Bent Street was the government's front yard, and the government kept its front yard in the
  sun" is not invention — it is the historical situation, relocated upward.

## Ellis Bent — the namesake

- Arrived Sydney **1 January 1810** aboard HMS *Dromedary* — **the same ship as Governor
  Macquarie**. The street's namesake and the street's namer shared a cabin wall for seven
  months at sea, which explains both the honour and how personally the falling-out was taken.
- **The colony's first professionally trained barrister** to hold judicial office. His
  predecessor as Judge-Advocate, Richard Atkins, was notorious for dispensing justice while
  drunk (reputation per the Australian Dictionary of Biography tradition — softer, but widely
  attested). The bar Bent had to clear was, literally, the bar.
- **He complained about his housing until the Governor built him a better house** — Macquarie
  had a brick residence put up for him (by Macquarie Place, completed June 1812), Bent having
  judged his position worthy of somewhat better accommodation. The judge extracted a house
  from the government before he began ruling against it. This is very Ulyssia.
- **The feud that named the street "bent."** Macquarie held that Bent's commission obliged him
  to obey *all* the Governor's orders; Bent held he need only obey the *lawful* ones — the
  colony's first fight over judicial independence. It ended with the Governor and the
  Judge-Advocate not speaking, each writing to London demanding the other's recall. London
  sided with Macquarie; Bent died **10 November 1815**, aged about 32, before the recall
  reached him — leaving four children and a widow pregnant with the fifth.
- **Even his grave got moved by the government.** Buried in the Old Sydney Burial Ground (the
  site now under Sydney Town Hall); relocated to **Garden Island in 1823**. The man who
  insisted on due process was exhumed by administrative convenience.
- **The brother.** Jeffery Hart Bent, Ellis's brother, arrived 1814 as **the first judge of
  the Supreme Court of Civil Judicature** — and refused for months to open his own court
  (he would not sit while ex-convict attorneys could appear). Australia's first supreme court
  judge was also its first judicial strike. He too was recalled. Two Bent brothers, both
  judges, both fell out with the government of New South Wales; only one got a street, and
  the government got to choose which.

## Dimensions and line of the real street

- **Length: ~330 m** of roadway (sum of centreline segments, OpenStreetMap). Short — five
  small blocks.
- **Line:** from **Macquarie Street** on the ridge at the eastern (top) end, westward and
  downhill: across **Phillip Street** (at what is now **Chifley Square**), across **Bligh
  Street**, across **O'Connell Street** (where the dogleg kinks), petering out at the
  Gresham/Loftus corner near Macquarie Place. The 1859 view from Phillip Street looks *down*
  Bent Street, past the three-storey Pulteney Hotel, to the (since-demolished) Macquarie
  fountain — i.e. the street had a closed vista at each end: ridge above, fountain below.
- **Width:** four lanes of carriageway at its widest today; building-face to building-face on
  the order of **20 m** (estimate from modern geometry, not a sourced figure — colonial
  streets of the 1810 regulation era were of the same order).
- **Chifley Square** is not original: in the **1860s** the section of Phillip Street between
  Bent and Hunter was realigned to connect with Elizabeth Street, and the resulting funnel of
  a junction was eventually dignified with a name (Ben Chifley's, in the 1940s) and a curved
  frontage. The street has been re-bent at least once since it was Bent.
- The 6 October 1810 proclamation that named it also divided the town into **five police
  districts with watchhouses** — the street was born already under observation, which the
  Directorate of Moral Observance would appreciate.

## Very early buildings and their functions

- **1789–1845: the Governor's garden.** First Government House stood at Bridge and Phillip
  Streets from 1789 until demolition in 1845–46; in front of the house lay a garden of
  imported species and **the colony's first orchard**. The garden and grounds ran back toward
  Bent Street's line, so the street's early decades had government ground along the north
  side and almost nothing to buy. *(Confidence: the garden-abuts-Bent-Street reading is
  inferred from the subdivision timing and early-map tradition rather than a single verbatim
  source — verify against the 1822/1836 town maps before making it load-bearing on page.)*
  **Care flag:** Arabanoo, the Aboriginal man held at Government House, was buried in that
  garden in May 1789. Real ground, real grave — not joke material; know it's there.
- **1845–46: the subdivision.** Government House was demolished, **the bricks sold at
  auction**, the garden and grounds cut into streets and allotments and sold; Phillip and
  Macquarie Streets were extended across the foundations to the new Circular Quay. The
  government's front yard went under the hammer — the exact inverse of Occupy: the public
  didn't seize the government's yard, the government liquidated it.
- **The Pulteney Hotel** (trading by 1834 under Thomas Petty, advertising "superior wines,
  spirits, bottled ales and port"): three storeys, the fashionable hotel of the street. From
  **1838** it housed the first clubhouse of the **Australian Club** (leased 1838, freehold
  1840) — the oldest gentlemen's club in Australia began as rooms over a pub on Bent Street.
- **The Australian Subscription Library**, corner of Bent and Macquarie, purpose-built
  1844–45 — on ground released by the Government House subdivision, which is why the corner
  was available at all. The library had been founded 1826–27 in rented rooms; on Bent Street
  it went broke with dignity until the government **bought it in 1869** and reopened it as
  the Sydney Free Public Library → Public Library of NSW → today's **State Library**. So the
  State Library of New South Wales began life on Bent Street as a private subscription club
  that failed upward into a public institution. Building demolished 1969.
- **The Union Club**, founded 1857 in a split from the Australian Club, settled at the
  Bligh/Bent corner; its 1950s curved-brick clubhouse (25 Bent Street) was **the last
  purpose-built city clubhouse in Australia**. The Australian Club, meanwhile, built its
  grand second clubhouse 1889–91 at the Bent/Macquarie corner — still there. A 330-metre
  street that at one point carried *both* of the colony's duelling gentlemen's clubs.

## Who and what used the street

The street has had exactly one tenant since 1810 — power — in successive costumes:

1. **The Governor** (1789–1845): garden, orchard, guardhouse; the public kept out.
2. **The gentlemen** (1830s–): the Pulteney Hotel, the clubs, professional town addresses;
   the public kept out politely. *(Soft: the residential-gentry phase is attested by the
   hotel/club record rather than a house-by-house source.)*
3. **The readers** (1845–1942): subscription library, then free public library — the one
   interval when the street's principal building let anybody in.
4. **The money** (20th c.–): insurance and banking, then the towers — and on the old library
   corner the government came back as landlord: the **State Office Block** ("the Black
   Stump," 30-odd storeys of 1960s state government) rose in 1967 and fell in 1997 for
   **Aurora Place**. Chifley Tower now stands at the head of the street; Deutsche Bank Place
   at the Phillip corner. The clubs, note, are still there — the street's longest continuous
   users are the gentlemen.

Never a market street, never a mob street — until the fiction supplies the mob.

## Narration-ready angles (the register, not the prose)

Seeds for humorous exposition, in the comic-grotesque register of the drafted scene — each is
a real fact wearing a funny hat, which is the house method (cf. the respectful-lampoon rule in
[[elvis-gospel-milieu]]: lampoon the apparatus, keep the ground true):

- The street is named for the one judge in the colony who would only obey lawful orders, and
  it bends. Nothing else on the street has been that honest since.
- The namesake and the Governor arrived on the same boat, the Governor built him a house, and
  within five years they communicated exclusively through London. The street commemorates the
  friendship; the bend commemorates the rest.
- Both Bent brothers defied the government; the government named a street after the dead one.
  There is a lesson in that for every occupier on the plaza.
- The government kept its front yard on Bent Street for thirty-five years, and when it
  finally opened the yard to the public it did so by auction. Occupy Bent Street is the
  refund queue, two-and-a-bit centuries late.
- Everything respectable on the street began as something else: the oldest club in the
  country began over a pub; the State Library began as a subscription racket that went broke;
  the public got a library because the gentlemen couldn't pay their dues. Institutions on
  Bent Street are laundered, not founded.
- The street was born under observation — named in the same proclamation that gave the town
  its five watch-districts — so Tramp Tower's floors of monitors are simply the watchhouse,
  gone vertical with everything else.
- Even the namesake's grave was relocated by the government; on Bent Street not even the dead
  hold their ground. (Pairs with the vigil's whole point being *staying*.)

## Bent Street Plaza — dimensions ruling (PROPOSED; author to adopt or amend)

Canon constraints: the plaza is the above-Layer, part-duplicated/part-redesigned frontage of
the ground street (geography.md, two-level CBD rule + Bent Street entry); it sits at
Airborne "ground," the lowest occupied level above the Layer; it is "acoustically engineered
for motorcades"; row order sunward→head: Moral Observance, Freedom, Housing, Food
Preservation Ombudsman, **Tramp Tower** at the head.

**The real footprint, for scale:** ~330 m × ~20 m ≈ **6,600 m²**. At rock-concert crush
(~3 people/m²) the real street tops out under **20,000 people**. Sty News's "forty thousand
known agitators exactly" would not physically fit on the real street; a quarter of a million
would not fit in the real precinct.

**Proposed ruling:** the plaza keeps the ground street's **line** — the ~330 m run and the
**dogleg** — but is *redesigned* (per canon) as a plaza roughly **70 m wide** along the
government frontages: call it **~23,000 m²**, with Tramp Tower closing the vista at the head
exactly as Chifley Tower closes the real street.

What those numbers buy the comedy, deliberately:

- **Vigil density** (kitchens, yurt, couch, karate room — ~1 person per 2 m²): **10–12,000**
  in comfort. "A few thousand of them at first" fits with sprawl to spare.
- **Standing crush**: **45–50,000** absolute ceiling. Against the drafted scene's competing
  crowd figures (scene `7F9C84D7`: Sty News's "forty thousand known agitators exactly" versus
  a Cloudborne stream's "a quarter of a million souls and rising"), the proposed area makes
  the forty thousand just barely physically possible and the quarter-million flatly
  impossible. Provenance: the figures are the drafted scene's; the feasibility comparison is
  derived from this ruling's area arithmetic and is documented nowhere else — it holds only
  if the ruling is adopted. Both figures stay "not within a mile of a count," as drafted.
- **The dogleg means the plaza cannot be seen end-to-end from the ground** — no occupier, no
  guard, and no reporter can ever count the crowd, only the choppers can, and the choppers
  work for the bulletins. The bend is load-bearing for the crowd-count comedy.

**If adopted:** record dimensions + dogleg in geography.md under the Bent Street entry,
alongside the existing access ruling of 2026-07-24.
