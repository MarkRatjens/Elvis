## Mandate

The Character Warden holds characters accountable to psychological consistency, legible desire, earned moments, and distinct function within the cast. At First Draft level: are the characters behaving like people, and are their desires visible on the page?

---

## Scene 1 — Occupy Bent Street

### Chanta

Chanta is the scene's most developed individual and the chapter's only named character who is both present on the page and emotionally consequential. His arc within the scene is small and complete — arrives wanting anonymity, is thrust into visibility, attempts to use visibility to correct a misreading of his own lyric, fails, retreats — and the arc is well-drawn at the level of action. The characterisation earns real confidence in several places.

**What works:**

The four minutes of anonymity he actively likes is the scene's most efficient piece of characterisation — a single detail that tells us he is not performing his own fame, that the myth has grown away from the man, and that he knows it. Joining the queue at its proper end is in the same register: he is not acting the Great Teacher; he is acting Chanta, and the two are different things.

The menu board — EVERYTHING. NO SUBSTITUTIONS — is a small joke that doubles as a character beat. He reads it and it satisfies him. This is a man who prefers reality to myth, and the menu board says: reality is not complicated, and it will not negotiate.

The interiority during the lyric-correction sequence is the scene's best writing at the character level: *blood rise, jaw locking, the sensation of lifting off the stage, wanting to vomit, drinking more beer*. These are physical renderings of a specific emotional state — humiliation compounded by powerlessness compounded by the discovery that being the author grants you no authority — and they are precise.

**What is underdeveloped:**

After the frontman leads him off stage, Chanta "searched deep within himself, found some resolve, and turned back. Then he lifted one of the eskies and strode off stage." The beat is right in outline — he almost says something, decides against it, takes the beer — but *found some resolve* is placeholder interiority. Resolve toward what? He turns back. He faces the crowd. What is he about to do? The reader needs to feel the edge of that moment before he turns away from it. The esky exit works precisely because it deflates something — but we need to feel what it deflates. Currently the beat is described from the outside rather than rendered from the inside, and it is the scene's most important character moment.

One possibility: the resolve is toward singing his lyric correctly himself — and he stops because he realises they wouldn't hear it any differently from him than from anyone else. That reading is available but not confirmed by the text.

**Desire:** Legible — he wants to be heard accurately, and he is not. This is a clean, small, painful desire, and it carries. The scene earns it.

### The Elvi (ensemble)

The Elvi function well and consistently as a collective character. Their adherence to the first little law — answering the press only in the Great Teacher's words — is the chapter's most elegant demonstration of collective characterisation: a shared constraint that produces a shared voice, which then produces a shared behaviour (the rendition that makes the sound recordist weep). The Elvi-as-chorus is working.

Individual Elvi are differentiated only by function (Elvine at the grill, vocalist on stage, honour guard). The Elvine who exclaims *"Great Teacher!"* and freezes with her tongs mid-air is the most vivid individual because she has a physical action that captures a state — the tongs suspended between grill and plate, the stammer, the default to *how do you want your eggs?* — and it is a genuinely funny and human moment. She is also the most tracked individual in the scene: she appears at the grill answering Chanta, she appears in the Sty News footage (turning sausages, practising karate kicks, contented as a woman in her own backyard), and she appears in the evening bulletins described identically. She has become, without being named, the scene's emblematic Elvi.

This is worth flagging as a structural opportunity: she is the character through whom the reader sees the occupation, and she is unnamed. Her fate in Scene 3 — if she is among the Elvi loaded into quarantine vehicles — is the chapter's most available emotional payload, and it currently cannot be delivered because there is no name to connect her across scenes.

**The High Elvis** arrives after the scene's dramatic climax (Dunhilton, riot squads) and is given a single paragraph of establishment — Elvi rising before eleven as the single biggest insurance risk of furniture and bathroom fixture shootings — that functions as a tonal breath and a credentialing of Elvis-world rules rather than as a character introduction. He plays no further role in the chapter. His presence at the occupation is noted but unexploited. This is acceptable at First Draft level if Scene 2 (*The Monday Conversation*) is where he acts, but he should not have a ceremonial introduction in Scene 1 if he has no function in Scene 1.

### The Airborne kids

These are characterised effectively as a type — the children of wealth encountering rougher pleasures and community for the first time — and a few rise briefly above type. The boy who pays for the T-shirts and is embarrassed by the thought of charging is a genuine character moment: it is a specific, unexpected response to wealth that says something true about a certain kind of privileged kid encountering generosity. The fourteen-year-old who explains the federation of the eskies with such fluency that two channels run him as a ringleader is funnier and also specific.

Neither is named. If either appears again in the chapter or the broader story, the absence of a name is a missed hook. If neither appears again, the anonymity is probably right — they are figures of the crowd, not characters in the plot.

### Dunhilton (Scene 1)

Dunhilton appears only in voice, via the televised address, and he is characterised with considerable skill. The key to his Scene 1 characterisation is that he is not angry. He is explaining. The worldview enacted by his language — *decent people, loitering, institutionally disentitled, lured from their stations, not one more layabout* — is a coherent ideology, delivered with the patience of a man who has said these things before and expects to have to say them again. He is not performing contempt; he holds it as a settled belief. This makes him more frightening than bluster would.

*"You do not negotiate with a picnic"* is the line that completes his characterisation: it reduces the occupation's entire claim to dignity to an inconvenience of scale, and does so with a phrase that is almost funny. A man who can say that without irony is a man without the faculty that would allow irony. This is well-observed.

## Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

### Gerry Hardman

This is the chapter's primary character problem, and it is serious enough at First Draft level to name clearly.

Gerry authorises a targeted biological attack against several thousand people, including children of shareholders. He does so in a secure laboratory, over a phone, with a single typed word. He then watches from his office window. This is the chapter's central act of evil, and Gerry is the agent of it. Scene 3 is his scene — he is the POV character from first paragraph to last.

And Gerry has no interior life visible on the page.

He is described as "betraying nothing of his internal calculations." He is observed "with clinical detachment." He makes "a final note in the operation log." His spoken dialogue consists of: *"Understood, sir," "Thank you, sir," "And the second phase, sir?"* and the single-word authorisation *"Proceed."* He does not think anything on the page. He does not feel anything on the page. He does not want anything on the page.

This is a character choice that can work — the bureaucrat-as-monster, the banality of administrative evil — but it is a choice that places enormous demands on the surrounding prose to carry the weight the character will not. And the prose in Scene 3 is also flat and procedural. The result is a character who is opaque for no dramatic payoff: he is not chilling, because the prose does not generate chill; he is not tragic, because nothing suggests there is anything tragic to lose; he is not comprehensible as a human being making a monstrous decision, because his interiority is absent.

The one detail that could open a door into Gerry is buried in the scene's second paragraph and never returned to: *he spent the previous night reviewing and revising his recommendation to deploy.* This is the most psychologically interesting thing in Scene 3. He revised it. Why? What did the original recommendation say that he changed? Did he tighten the constraints? Loosen them? Add the Airborne exception? Remove it? The revision is where Gerry's character lives — the moment before the scene began when a person weighed something and made a choice — and it is currently off-page.

**Three possible Gerrys, all available from the current text, none confirmed:**

1. **The true believer.** Gerry genuinely holds the ideology. The Elvi are a genetic aberration; the deployment is a public health measure; the operation log note is sincere. His detachment is not repression but conviction. This Gerry is frightening in a specific way — he is not suppressing his conscience, he has replaced it.

2. **The careerist.** Gerry has long since stopped asking whether orders are right and asks only whether they are executable. He revised the recommendation because the original had operational vulnerabilities, not moral ones. His detachment is professional habit. He is not a monster; he is a function. This Gerry is the most common kind of perpetrator and the hardest to write without making him boring.

3. **The knowing participant.** Gerry knows exactly what he is doing and does it anyway, for reasons the text has not yet supplied — fear, debt, complicity in earlier operations, love of something that depends on his continued employment. His detachment is performed, not felt. He spends the night revising the recommendation because he is looking for a reason not to proceed, and does not find one. This Gerry has the most available tragedy.

None of these is currently on the page. The scene needs to commit to one.

**Desire:** Illegible. This is the character's primary diagnostic problem.

### Dunhilton (Scene 3)

In Scene 3, Dunhilton's voice has shifted register from Scene 1 in ways that weaken rather than develop his characterisation.

Scene 1 Dunhilton is dry, explanatory, confident. He does not call people genetic freaks on television; he calls them "the institutionally disentitled." He does not rage; he regrets. His ideology is stated in the language of civic responsibility and natural social order. This is the voice of a man who believes what he says, and that belief is precisely what makes him dangerous — he is not performing cruelty, he is administering it.

Scene 3 Dunhilton is "almost cheerful" on the phone. He says "genetic freaks dropping like flies." He describes Channel Forty-Eight's coverage as "beautiful misdirection." He tells Gerry history will remember the day. He congratulates his board.

This is a different character. Scene 1 Dunhilton would not say "genetic freaks" — not because he doesn't think it, but because he would never use language that expressive. Scene 1 Dunhilton uses the language of order, not the language of contempt; contempt would be beneath him. Scene 3 Dunhilton is performing villainy for an audience (Gerry, the board, himself), and performing villainy is less frightening than inhabiting it.

The most useful question for revision: does Dunhilton know he is a villain? Scene 1 suggests he does not — he genuinely believes himself to be defending civilisation. Scene 3 suggests he might — he is gleeful in a way that implies awareness of transgression. These are incompatible characterisations, and the more effective one is Scene 1.

### The lead scientist

He is nervous. We know this because he is described as nervous and because he keeps adjusting his protective goggles. We do not know *why* he is nervous. Technical anxiety (will the deployment work?) and moral anxiety (is this right?) are both consistent with the goggles-adjusting, but they produce entirely different characters. If he is technically nervous, he is a competent man worried about his competence. If he is morally nervous, he is the scene's available conscience — the figure who could ask the question nobody asks, and doesn't. At this draft stage the nervousness is under-motivated and the opportunity it represents is unexploited.

## Hard Craft Question

Gerry Hardman is on the page for almost the entirety of Scene 3. He authorises targeted mass killing. He watches it from his window. He types one word. He files a note. **Which kind of person does this — true believer, professional non-asker, or knowing participant — and where exactly in Scene 3 does the reader find out?** If the answer is *nowhere*, the scene is not yet written from the inside.
