## Mandate

The Dialogue Tuner listens to every line of spoken language, spoken-to-crowd utterance, chanted lyric used dramatically, and dramatic silence. It asks: does this line do work that only this character can do? Does the exchange move, reveal, or complicate something? Is the line alive on the page — could it be spoken aloud by a human mouth — or is it functional furniture? At First Draft level: is the spoken language earning its place, and are the registers distinct?

---

## Scope note: what counts as dialogue in this chapter

This chapter is unusual in that its most consequential "dialogue" is not two characters speaking in a room. It operates in three registers simultaneously:

1. **Attributed spoken lines** — Chanta's stage sequence, the Elvine's *"How do you want your eggs?"*, the frontman's encouragement, the press exchanges, the Gerry/Dunhilton phone calls.
2. **Crowd chant and lyric as dramatic action** — the repeated lines of *Religious Minds* function as dialogue between Chanta and the crowd: he speaks, the crowd replies in a language it has already decided on. These are the most dramatically loaded "exchanges" in Scene 1.
3. **Reported speech** — Dunhilton's televised address, the news reporters' questions, the Elvi's sung answers to the press.

All three require evaluation. The Dialogue Tuner treats the crowd's chorus lines as dialogue, because that is their dramatic function.

## Scene 1 — Occupy Bent Street

### The Chanta stage sequence

This is the chapter's best piece of dramatic dialogue writing. It works because it is built as a collision, not a conversation: Chanta speaks in prose and the crowd replies in lyric, and they are permanently talking past each other. The form enacts the content.

**What is working:**

*"Would like to come back to my place and see my floor polishers?"* is the scene's best single line. It is funny in a specific way — the syntax has gone wrong (*Would* not *Would you*), the register is absurdly domestic, and it is delivered into a microphone to tens of thousands of people while the speaker is trying to ask the frontman a private question. The line does three things: it characterises Chanta as someone who defaults to awkward sincerity under pressure; it shows that he has forgotten, in the panic, that the microphone is live; and it makes the scene's emotional logic coherent — he is a man who runs a cleaning service, not a prophet, and the gap between those two things is the whole joke. The crowd cheering it wildly is funnier than the line itself.

*"I mean… I run Enchanted Cleaning Services. No job too small, or murder too messy."* — the recovery is good. The self-deprecation is real rather than performed. The ellipsis and false start (*"I mean…"*) are correctly placed: he is not winding up to a punchline; he is retreating to something he knows.

The lyric-correction attempt:

> *"No… no—that's… that's not… it's we can't go on."*
> *"It's, 'We won't praise the Dawkins'. 'We won't praise the Dawkins with religious minds.'"*

Both of these lines are rendered at the right level of fragmentation. He is not articulate under pressure. The dashes and ellipses and the repetition of the corrected line are the voice of a man who knows what he means and cannot make it land, which is precisely the condition of the scene. No note here — these are doing exactly what they should.

**One technical issue: the beer line.**

*"I really go a beer"* — the typo (*go* for *want*) is almost certainly a draft error, not a speech pattern, but it needs to be confirmed either way. If Chanta has a characteristic dropped or transposed word in his speech, this could be seeded earlier. If it is a typo, it should be corrected before this scene is evaluated for voice.

**The recovery beat: *"Can I get you something?"***

*"I really go a beer,"* Chanta croaks into the mic. The frontman's question is perfectly judged — it is an attempt to normalise, a social grace extended in the middle of catastrophe — and the crowd's response (*Papal-Mass-level-wild*) is the scene's best comic payoff because it converts Chanta's plea for alcohol into a messianic gesture. This line works.

**The instruction to try karate** (*"Next time, try some karate"*) is slightly over-functional. It is there to seed the karate motif, and its placement is correct (just before the esky exit), but it is the one line in the frontman's mouth that feels authored rather than spoken. A man managing a celebrity meltdown in front of tens of thousands of people would probably say something more instinctively placating. *"Next time, try some karate"* is funny but slightly too aware of itself. Consider whether the karate callback earns the slight disruption to the frontman's voice.

### The Elvine's line: *"Great Teacher! How do you want your eggs?"*

This is the best-constructed short exchange in the chapter. The exclamation and the question exist in entirely different registers — reverence and practical hospitality — and the collision between them is the scene's first genuine comedy. The stammer (*she stammered*) between the exclamation and the question earns its place: it is the exact physical response of someone whose ideology and their job have collided without warning.

Chanta's reply — *"Hard-scrambled"* — is perfect. It is the correct answer to the wrong question: the crowd expects a revelation, and it gets an egg preference. The word *hard-scrambled* does additional work because it is the exact register of the occupation's food (the eggs are already described as hard-scrambled later in the menu) — Chanta is ordering what everyone else has, not imposing a preference, and this is quietly in character.

### The press sequence: Elvi answering in Great Teacher's words

This is the chapter's most sustained piece of dialogue-as-comedy-of-constraint, and it is very good. The structure is clear — question in reported speech, answer in Great Teacher's lyric (also reported, or sung aloud) — and the escalation is correctly handled: a couplet about a hound dog, then the second verse, then the lonesomeness ballad that makes the sound recordist weep. The comedy builds because the Elvi are not being evasive; they are being scrupulously sincere. They are answering the questions; the answers just happen to be song lyrics.

One craft note: *Blake Mudrake* asks three questions to which he receives sung answers, and the questions are given in reported speech (*asked who had organised the occupation*, *asked what the movement's demands were*, *asked whether they would disperse if ordered to*). This is the right choice — attributing these questions as direct speech would slow the sequence and make Mudrake too present. But the third question and answer is the scene's best, and it is worth checking whether it is selling itself short: *asked whether they would disperse if ordered to, the Elvis consulted his colleagues, and all six gave Mudrake so tender a rendition of a ballad about lonesomeness that the sound recordist wept quietly into her headphones and the interview was abandoned.* The reporting of this moment is slightly coy. The sound recordist weeping is a wonderful detail but it is delivered in a subordinate clause. Consider whether this moment — the scene's most affecting reported dialogue beat — deserves to breathe a little more.

### Dunhilton's televised address

Dunhilton's address is the chapter's longest piece of uninterrupted direct speech, and it is the scene's most technically accomplished dialogue passage. It has been assessed in depth by the Character Warden for characterisation; the Dialogue Tuner adds the following craft observations.

**Register:** The address is written in the voice of a man who has learned to say monstrous things in civic language, and every line sustains that register. *"Loitering, with a sound system"* is the address's best line because it is structurally two clauses — the second a parenthetical afterthought — and the parenthetical carries more weight than the main clause. The move is elegant: he dismisses the occupation's scale (the sound system) by making it a footnote to the dismissal of its intent.

**The self-correction:** *"lured above their stations" — he corrected himself — "lured from their stations"* is excellent. It tells us that Dunhilton has a preferred class vocabulary — *stations*, not *place* — and that he self-monitors in real time. The correction is not toward truthfulness; it is toward precision. This is a man whose ideology has a grammar.

**The press questions:** The three questions Dunhilton is asked are each structurally correct as pressure tests: timeline, negotiation, and the political impossibility question. His answers — *timelines are commercially confidential*, *you do not negotiate with a picnic*, and the amphibious look that ends the briefing — are graduated perfectly. The third is the best because he does not answer it at all, and the journalist's inexperience is specified (*hadn't been at the Smear long enough to know better*) rather than implied. This is dialogue writing that understands how power handles a question it cannot answer.

**One note:** *"You did not build your towers so that just anyone could stand underneath them"* is Dunhilton's opening line, and it is the address's weakest. It is trying to be a memorable opener, and it is trying slightly too hard — the towers/underneath construction is a little literary for a man whose preferred register is civic bureaucracy and patrician patience. Compare it to *"you do not negotiate with a picnic"*, which is more dangerous precisely because it sounds so banal. The opener would benefit from being flatlined in the same way.

---

### Reporter with the peanut butter sandwich

*"It's very good," he told the nation* is one of the chapter's finest lines, and it is a piece of dialogue that does exactly what Scene 1 is about. The reporter goes looking for a spokesperson, a demand, a coherent message — and what he gets is a sandwich, and reports it to a nation as though it is news. That *"It's very good"* is delivered sincerely, on camera, is the scene's most efficient satire. No note.

The girl's non-answer — the reach into the esky, the sandwich — is also correct as a dramatic beat. She does not evade the question; she answers it at a different frequency, and the reporter receives it at that frequency. This exchange is doing genuine work about the incompatibility of institutional journalism and community-scale spontaneity, and it is doing it in two sentences and a reported action.

## Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

### Gerry/Dunhilton exchanges: the phone calls

The dialogue in Scene 3 is the chapter's most serious craft problem, and the Dialogue Tuner names it plainly.

Every line of spoken dialogue in Scene 3 is one of two things: **technical briefing** or **villainy narrated aloud**. Neither generates dramatic tension, because both are transparent — they exist to deliver information to the reader, not to perform a relationship between characters.

**The technical briefing problem:**

> *"Yes, Commissioner. The TMinus-Virus dispersal has been calibrated for this specific urban environment. The active radius will extend approximately two hundred meters from each deployment point, covering the entirety of the Occupy area."*

This is expository dialogue: a character explaining things the other character already knows, for the reader's benefit. The scientist addresses Gerry as *Commissioner* in the sentence immediately after explaining a technical parameter — no working technician briefs their superior with their title mid-sentence in a context of continuous conversation. The line is written for the reader, not for the scene.

A diagnostic test: *would this character say this, to this person, at this moment?* The scientist has calibrated this system. Gerry has presumably reviewed the technical specifications. Neither is learning this information in this room; they are confirming it. Confirmation dialogue sounds different from explanation dialogue — it is shorter, it is cagier, it proceeds by assumption rather than elaboration. The scene's technical exchanges currently have the grammar of a briefing to someone who has never encountered the subject.

**The villainy-narrated-aloud problem:**

> *"This is working out better than expected, actually. The public seeing these genetic freaks dropping like flies while normal citizens remain healthy? Couldn't ask for better optics regarding the Elvi threat."*

> *"You know, Gerry, history will remember this as the day we preserved Ulyssia's natural social order."*

These lines have the character warden's problem and the dialogue tuner's problem simultaneously. The character warden identifies that Dunhilton would not use language this expressive — this is correct, and it is also a dialogue problem. But there is an additional issue: both lines are characters telling each other what is happening and what it means. *"History will remember this as the day we preserved Ulyssia's natural social order"* is Dunhilton summarising the theme of the chapter for the reader. This is a version of a character saying *"As you know, Bob"* — except what Dunhilton is explaining to Gerry is not information Gerry needs, but ideology Gerry already holds (or doesn't, but we can't tell). The line exists because the author wants the reader to know what this operation means ideologically. That is legitimate. But it cannot be delivered through a character's direct speech without making the character into a vessel for authorial statement.

The most effective villains do not narrate their villainy. They speak about ordinary things while enacting extraordinary evil. Dunhilton's Scene 1 address works because he is talking about civic order and social responsibility while authorising the containment of an occupation. His Scene 3 phone calls do not work because he is talking about his own villainy while enacting it.

**Gerry's dialogue:**

Gerry speaks nine times in Scene 3. His lines:
- *"You're certain about containment parameters?"*
- *"And the genetic targeting?"*
- *"Understood, sir."*
- *"We're prepared to deploy immediately."*
- *"Deploy at the five designated points. Synchronize for maximum coverage."* *"Time to dispersal: thirty minutes."*
- *"Thank you, sir."*
- *"We should have the area cleared within hours. The illness appears severe enough to warrant hospitalization for affected individuals."*
- *"And the second phase, sir?"*
- *"Proceed."*

Every line in this list is either a question seeking technical confirmation, a deferential acknowledgement, or a command. Not one of them is a statement of Gerry's own position, a moment of hesitation, an edge of something. The Character Warden has identified this as the scene's central problem; the Dialogue Tuner confirms it from a different angle: there is nothing in Gerry's spoken language that tells us who is speaking these words. Any functionary with the rank of Commissioner could deliver this list. The dialogue has no voice because the character has no interiority to generate one.

The one exception — and it is the right instinct even if it doesn't fully pay off — is *"We should have the area cleared within hours. The illness appears severe enough to warrant hospitalization for affected individuals."* This line has a slightly different texture from the others, because *warrant hospitalization* is bureaucratic cover-language — he is shaping the narrative as he speaks, reaching for institutional deniability. It is the only line in Gerry's list that suggests a mind managing both the operation and its framing simultaneously. It is the direction to develop.

### What the chapter's dialogue does well: the crowd-as-interlocutor

The most structurally original dialogue technique in this chapter is the use of the crowd's chorus as a dramatic respondent. In the Chanta stage sequence, the crowd sings back wrong lines, and the collision between what Chanta says and what the crowd insists on singing is the scene's dramatic action. This is a genuine formal discovery: collective misreading enacted as dialogue.

This is the chapter's strongest dialogue innovation and it should be named as such. The technique is particularly effective because it cannot be resolved — Chanta cannot correct thousands of people, and the crowd is not being defiant; it is being sincere. The crowd is singing what it understood. The gap between authorial intention and received meaning is performed rather than explained, and it is performed through the form of dialogue.

The lyric-as-dialogue technique in the opening section of Scene 1 — where the crowd fragments into competing factions singing different lines and then coalesces into one — is also doing structural dialogue work: the moment of coalescence (*And all the mobs found themselves singing as one*) is the chapter's first image of the coalition that Scene 3 will destroy.

The inventory reveals a structural problem: the chapter's most distinctive voice (Chanta), its most effective dialogue technique (crowd-as-respondent), and its best individual exchanges (Elvine, reporter with sandwich, Elvi answering press in lyric) are all in Scene 1. Scene 3 replaces living speech with operational dialogue and phone villainy. The tonal gap between the two scenes is extreme enough that they do not currently feel like the same chapter's dialogue.

## Line-level notes: minor issues

1. **"I really go a beer"** — confirm typo vs. speech pattern. If typo, correct. If pattern, seed earlier.
2. **"Would like to come back to my place"** — the dropped *you* is almost certainly intentional (panic syntax) but could be ambiguous on the page. One word of setup — even *he asked the frontman* (marking it as addressed away from the mic) — would clarify this as accident rather than error.
3. **The crowd's Song text set off mid-section** — the chapter uses lyric stanzas as dialogue throughout. The stanza formatting is consistent in Scene 1. In Scene 3, there is no equivalent use of formal or lyric register. This is not an error (there is no singing in Scene 3) but it reinforces how fully the chapter's tonal and formal registers have been separated between the two scenes.
4. **"An honour guard escorted him from the kerb, between a florist's trolley and a pallet of portaloo chemicals"** — this is narration, not dialogue, but it earns a note: this sentence is the best physical comedy in the chapter. The specific cargo (florist's trolley, portaloo chemicals) is the right kind of absurdist grounding. No action required; note for voice-level appreciation at polish stage.

## Strongest dialogue moments (Scene 1)

Ranked by dramatic function:

1. *"Hard-scrambled."* — registers collapse, the myth meets the man.
2. *"It's very good,"* the reporter told the nation — institutional form surrenders to spontaneous reality.
3. Dunhilton's self-correction: *"lured above their stations" → "lured from their stations"* — ideology with a grammar.
4. The lyric-correction sequence — crowd-as-dialogue, misreading performed.
5. *"You do not negotiate with a picnic."* — banality as contempt; contempt as worldview.
6. The lonesomeness ballad that makes the sound recordist weep — the press interview abandoned to a better frequency.

## Hard Craft Question

Scene 3's dialogue could be spoken by any Commissioner, any CEO, any nervous scientist in any thriller involving a covert operation. **Remove every character's name from Scene 3 and read the spoken lines aloud: is there a single line that could only come from Gerry Hardman, and not from a generic functionary?** If the answer is no — and currently it is — Scene 3 has not yet found its characters' voices, and no amount of action or event will substitute for that.
