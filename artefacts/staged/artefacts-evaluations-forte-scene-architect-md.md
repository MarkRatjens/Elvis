# `artefacts/evaluations/forte-scene-architect.md`

## Mandate

The Scene Architect evaluates structural integrity: does each scene have a clear dramatic engine, a turn, and a landing? Does the chapter sequence build? Are scenes doing distinct narrative work, or doubling up?

---

## Scene 1 — Occupy Bent Street

### What the scene is doing

This is an accretion scene — a crowd accretes around a spontaneous occupation, and what accretes with it is tone, community, and comic texture. Structurally it operates as a series of escalating comic tableaux (blankets, couches crowd-surfed like hippopotamuses, the yurt at half-mast, the Elvine turning sausages while practising karate kicks) punctuated by two harder dramatic beats: Chanta's stage appearance, and Dunhilton's address to the nation.

### Structural engine

The scene lacks a single dramatic engine in the conventional sense — there is no protagonist pursuing a goal against resistance within this scene. Instead it runs on cumulative momentum: the occupation keeps growing, keeps improvising, keeps becoming more itself. That is a legitimate structural choice for a crowd scene of this kind, but it places enormous weight on the two punctuation points (Chanta, Dunhilton) to supply the dramatic charge that a through-line would otherwise carry.

**Chanta's sequence** does have an engine: he arrives incognito and wants a quiet beer; he is thrust onto stage; he tries to correct the crowd's misreading of his lyric and fails; he retreats with an esky. That arc is small but clean — arrival → exposure → attempt → defeat → retreat. It works. The esky exit is a satisfying beat.

**Dunhilton's address** is structurally elegant: he announces containment, the crowd immediately interprets it as a door policy they've won, and the scene ends with the split-screen image — faith on one side, revolt on the other, the same song leaking across. That is a genuine structural landing.

### What is missing or weak

The scene has **two endings**: the Dunhilton split-screen close, and then a second, quieter coda — the night guard waving, the marshmallows, the final image of OCCUPY BENT STREET reading as a forwarding address. The coda is the better ending — warmer, more resonant, earns the chapter's emotional register — but it comes *after* what feels like the scene's natural close. This creates a structural rhythm problem: the reader has already landed once before being asked to land again.

The **riot squad arrival** is embedded in the coda rather than given its own beat. It deserves more structural weight — the moment the state deploys armed officers against people eating peanut-butter sandwiches and waving at windows is the scene's darkest note. Currently it is handled in a single paragraph and immediately softened by the sergeant eating the sausage. That softening is tonally right, but the beat passes too quickly to register as the turning point it structurally is.

The **Sunday afternoon / T-shirts section** feels structurally loose — it is a bundle of good comic details in search of a spine. The Cloudborne/Airborne T-shirt collaboration (one with anger, one with capital) is the best detail and should probably anchor the section more explicitly as its structural beat, since it enacts the theme (coalition across class) in miniature.

## Scene 2 — The Monday Conversation

**No content supplied. Title only.**

This is a structural gap in the chapter. A chapter with three scenes where the middle scene is absent cannot be evaluated for chapter-level arc. The Monday Conversation presumably carries the plot consequence of the occupation — a negotiation, a decision, a confrontation — and its absence means the chapter currently jumps from community/celebration (Scene 1) directly to biological attack (Scene 3) without the narrative hinge that should connect them.

**Structural risk:** Without Scene 2, Operation Cleanse arrives with no dramatic preparation. The reader has not been inside the power structure watching the decision to deploy; they have only seen the community side. Scene 2 is therefore load-bearing for the chapter's overall arc.

## Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

### What the scene is doing

This scene enacts the chapter's turn from comedy/community to horror. Structurally it is a countdown scene: deployment authorised → deployment executed → symptoms manifest → chaos → Dunhilton's approval call → aftermath. The structure is clear and the escalation is competent.

### Structural concerns

**Register collision.** Scene 1 is written in a voice of exuberant comic richness — crowd-surfed couches, fool's gold loaf, the yurt at half-mast. Scene 3 is written in a register that is flat, procedural, and thriller-adjacent. This is not inherently wrong — tonal whiplash can be deliberately deployed — but at First Draft level the collision is too absolute. There is no bridging device, no voice continuity, nothing that says *this is the same story told from the other side of the glass*. The shift reads like a genre transplant rather than a deliberate tonal turn.

**Point of view and interiority.** Gerry is the POV character, but his interiority is almost entirely absent. We are told his face betrays nothing; we are told he makes final notes; we are told he observes with clinical detachment. This is a man authorising a genocide. The scene is structurally doing enormous work — it is the chapter's horror pivot — and it is doing it with a character who has no discernible internal life. At this draft stage the structure holds, but the character engine is stalled.

**Dunhilton's phone calls** serve as exposition delivery and villain-confirmation, but they are structurally redundant — they tell us what we are already watching. Both calls arrive at moments when the dramatic action (symptoms manifesting, protesters being segregated) is more powerful than anything being said over the phone. The calls interrupt the scene's own momentum.

**The ending** is thematically clear (instruments abandoned, the last image of what was) but structurally anticlimactic — it describes the aftermath rather than landing on an action or image that reverberates. Compare with Scene 1's ending: the forwarding-address image earns its resonance by recontextualising something we've already seen. Scene 3's ending introduces new description at the close rather than turning existing material.

## Mandate

The Character Warden evaluates whether characters behave with psychological consistency, whether their desire is legible on the page, whether they earn their moments, and whether the cast is doing distinct work.

## Scene 1 — Occupy Bent Street

### Chanta

Chanta is the scene's most developed character and its most structurally important. His arc within the scene is small and complete: arrives wanting anonymity, gets thrust into visibility, tries to use the visibility to say something true, fails, leaves with the beer. The failure is characterised well — the crowd's mishearing of his lyric is a small devastating joke about the relationship between artists and their audiences, and Chanta's response (jaw locking, blood rising, the sensation of lifting off the stage, the impulse to vomit) is the scene's most specific interiority.

**What works:** The four minutes of anonymity he likes, the queue at its proper end, the menu board that says EVERYTHING. NO SUBSTITUTIONS — these details are in character and earn the reader's confidence in Chanta as a person who prefers reality to myth.

**What doesn't (yet):** After the failed correction of his lyric, Chanta is talked off stage, then turns back, "found some resolve," and then — takes an esky and leaves. The resolve is named but not dramatised. What does he almost say? What does he look for in the crowd? The beat is slightly underwritten for a character this central. The retreat with the esky is funny and probably right, but "found some resolve" is placeholder interiority — it tells us a character beat happened without letting us feel it.

**Desire:** Legible — he wants to be heard correctly. He is not heard correctly. He leaves.

### The Elvines / Elvi (ensemble)

The Elvi function well as a collective character throughout Scene 1. Their adherence to the first little law (answering press only in the Great Teacher's words) is beautifully handled — the rendition of the lonesomeness ballad that makes the sound recordist weep is one of the chapter's finest comic moments. As a group they are consistent and distinct.

Individual Elvi are not yet differentiated within the scene beyond function (Elvine at grill, vocalist on stage, honour guard). At this draft stage that is acceptable — the Elvi are correctly functioning as a chorus. But if any individual Elvi is to carry weight in later scenes, they need a name and a trait planted here.

### The Airborne kids

These are well-characterised as a type — the children of wealth encountering poverty-adjacent pleasure for the first time — but they risk becoming a comic device rather than characters. The boy who pays for the T-shirts and is embarrassed to have considered charging is a genuine character moment; the crocodile-ordering sequence is another. These individuals should be named or at least traced across the scene, because they are currently doing thematic work (class collision) that is not yet anchored to specific people we will recognise.

### Dunhilton

He exists only in voice (the TV address) but his voice is characterised sharply enough to establish him. "Loitering, with a sound system" is a good line — it tells us exactly who he is. His language enacts his worldview: he is not angry, he is explaining something obvious to people too dim to see it. This is more chilling than bluster would be.

## Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

### Gerry Hardman

This is the chapter's most significant character problem.

Gerry authorises a targeted biological attack on several thousand people at a public protest. This is genocide-adjacent action. The scene requires us to spend considerable time inside his perspective. But Gerry has no interiority — he is described as "betraying nothing," observing "with clinical detachment," making notes in a log. His only recorded internal state is "internal calculations" (unspecified) and the fact that he spent the previous night revising his recommendation.

This is a structural choice that can work — the banality-of-evil register, the bureaucrat as monster — but it requires the prose to carry what the character will not show. The prose at present is also flat and procedural. The result is a character who is opaque for no dramatic payoff. We don't fear him because we don't understand him. We don't find him chilling because the prose is too neutral to generate chill. We don't find him tragic because nothing suggests there is anything to lose.

**The one crack:** He spent the previous night reviewing and revising his recommendation. That is the most interesting thing about Gerry in the scene, and it is buried in the second paragraph and never returned to. *Why did he revise it? What did he add? What did he remove?* That revision is where his character lives, and it is currently off-page.

**Desire:** Illegible. Does Gerry want the deployment to succeed? Does he want it to fail? Does he want to be promoted? Does he want absolution? Does he want simply to do his job cleanly? The absence of legible desire is the scene's primary character problem.

### Dunhilton (Scene 3)

In Scene 3, Dunhilton has lost the dry authority of his televised address and become a stock villain — "almost cheerful," congratulating his board, talking about "beautiful misdirection" and "optics." The characterisation in Scene 1 was more dangerous because it was more mundane. Dunhilton at his most chilling is the man who genuinely believes what he says. Dunhilton on the phone in Scene 3 sounds like a man performing villainy, which is less frightening than a man performing civic virtue.

### The lead scientist

Named only as "a nervous man with thinning hair who kept adjusting his protective goggles." He is a figure of anxiety — nervous, adjusting goggles — but we don't know *why* he is nervous. Is he nervous about technical failure? About the morality of what he's doing? These are very different characterisations and both are available. At this draft stage the nervousness is under-motivated.

## Mandate

The Dialogue Tuner evaluates whether spoken words are doing distinct work from narration, whether voices are differentiated, whether subtext is present or absent, and whether dialogue earns its place in the scene's economy.

## Scene 1 — Occupy Bent Street

The dialogue in Scene 1 is sparse, wisely — this is fundamentally a narrated crowd scene, and long dialogue exchanges would fragment its accumulative rhythm. The dialogue that exists mostly works.

### Chanta on stage

The stage sequence is the scene's primary dialogue passage. It is working well, but unevenly.

**"Would like to come back to my place and see my floor polishers?"** — This is excellent. It is accidental (he's still talking into the mic), it is in character (he defaults to his real identity under pressure), and it generates the biggest laugh in the sequence while also being poignant. The crowd's response (they agree they would) is the right beat.

**"I mean… I run Enchanted Cleaning Services. No job too small, or murder too messy."** — The ellipsis recovery and the tagline read as rehearsed in a way that the floor-polisher line doesn't, even though logically it shouldn't be. The floor-polisher line sounds panicked; the cleaning-services line sounds like he's remembering his elevator pitch. The tonal inconsistency is small but present.

**"I just want to say … something … something about a song I wrote yesterday… Religious Minds?"** — The stammering fragmentation is right. The crowd interrupting each attempt is structurally sound. But Chanta's dialogue here is almost entirely interrupted, which means we never hear what he *would* say if allowed to finish. That could be deliberate (the point is that he can't), but it means the climax of the dialogue sequence is absence rather than utterance — which is more appropriate for a later, developed scene than a first draft.

**"No. No, please listen. You're singing it wrong."** — This is the truest line in the passage and the most painful. It lands.

### The frontman

The frontman's dialogue is functional but undifferentiated — he sounds like a generic MC. "Just tell them a little about yourself, like you would someone you meet at a bar" is competent but generic. "Next time, try some karate" is better — it's specific to this world. The frontman should have a more distinct voice if he's going to appear again; if this is his only scene, a single more specific line would fix him in memory.

### Dunhilton's address

This is the scene's most sustained dialogue passage and it is well-written. The rhetorical structure — *decent people / loiterers / impressionable children lured from their stations* — enacts his worldview in the way his word choices work. "Loitering, with a sound system" is the scene's best line. "You do not negotiate with a picnic" is equally good — it's funny and appalling simultaneously, which is exactly the right register for this character.

The three press questions are functional; the young journalist's question about shareholders' children is the best of them because it makes Dunhilton's silence (the "long, amphibious moment") more eloquent than any answer.

### The reporter and the sandwich

**"It's very good," he told the nation.** This is a perfect line. No notes.

### The first little law in practice

The Elvi answering the press in lyrics is handled entirely in narration rather than dialogue — we are told they answered with a couplet about a hound dog, told they gave a rendition of a ballad about lonesomeness. The choice not to render the actual dialogue (the actual lyrics) is correct — quoting the lyrics would shift register and create copyright considerations — but the narration is slightly thin as a result. "So tender a rendition of a ballad about lonesomeness that the sound recordist wept quietly into her headphones" is good narration, but it describes a dialogue moment without dramatising it. Consider whether a single word or two of quoted lyric (a fragment, not a full line) might anchor this more.

---

## Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

The dialogue in Scene 3 is the chapter's weakest writing, and it is weak primarily because it is doing work that narration or action should be doing instead.

### The scientist's briefing

**"The TMinus-Virus dispersal has been calibrated for this specific urban environment. The active radius will extend approximately two hundred meters from each deployment point, covering the entirety of the Occupy area."**

This is exposition dressed as dialogue. The scientist is telling Gerry things Gerry already knows, for the reader's benefit. The tell is the phrase "covering the entirety of the Occupy area" — a scientist briefing a commissioner in a secure lab would not explain the target geography to the man who approved the operation. He would use coordinates or shorthand. The explanation exists because the reader needs it, not because the character needs to say it.

The same problem recurs throughout: **"The virus will only activate in subjects with Elvi genetic markers. Others will experience mild cold-like symptoms at worst."** Gerry authorised this operation. He knows how it works. The dialogue is telling him his own plan.

### Dunhilton's phone calls

Both calls are composed almost entirely of villain-exposition: Dunhilton explains what is happening (which Gerry can see), confirms that it is going well, and adds ideological commentary ("history will remember this as the day we preserved Ulyssia's natural social order"). This is the dialogue equivalent of a villain monologue — it is explaining the stakes rather than enacting them.

**"Hospitalization? No, no—quarantine facilities have been prepared."** This is the most effective line in Scene 3's dialogue, because it introduces new information that changes the situation, is in character, and has subtext (the distinction between hospital and quarantine facility carries enormous weight). More dialogue like this, less recapitulation.

**"This is working out better than expected, actually. The public seeing these genetic freaks dropping like flies while normal citizens remain healthy? Couldn't ask for better optics regarding the Elvi threat."** The word "optics" in this context is accurate but the line is too on-the-nose — Dunhilton is articulating the ideology too cleanly. His Scene 1 voice was more dangerous because it was more oblique. He didn't call people genetic freaks on TV; he called them "the institutionally disentitled." Scene 3 Dunhilton is saying the quiet part loud, which makes him less frightening, not more.

### Gerry's dialogue

Gerry says almost nothing in Scene 3. His responses are: "Understood, sir." / "Thank you, sir." / "And the second phase, sir?" This is consistent with his characterisation as an opaque, bureaucratic figure — but it also means Scene 3 has almost no dialogue that creates genuine exchange. All information flows one direction: from scientist/Dunhilton to Gerry (and reader). Gerry never pushes back, deflects, asks a question for any reason other than to receive more exposition, or says anything that reveals his inner state. His single-word authorisation ("Proceed") is his best dialogue moment because it is the most compressed.

---

## Hard Craft Question

Scene 3's dialogue is almost entirely expository — characters telling each other (and the reader) things they already know. The scene's horror depends on what is *not* said, what Gerry does *not* react to, the silence under the bureaucratic language. **Is there a single line in Scene 3 that only Gerry could say — something that tells us who he is, not what he is doing?** If not, what would that line be?

### Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

**Critical continuity problem:** Scene 3's opening states "The protest above had entered its third day, growing larger with each passing hour." If the concert ends Saturday night/Sunday morning and Scene 1 covers Sunday, then Scene 3's "third day" would be Tuesday. But Scene 2 is titled *The Monday Conversation*, placing it on Monday. This means:

- **Scene 1 = Sunday** ✓ (consistent with "on a Sunday, the central business and political district was a ghost town")
- **Scene 2 = Monday** (title implies this)
- **Scene 3 = Tuesday** (third day of occupation)

This is internally coherent — the operation follows the Monday conversation — but it is not stated in Scene 3. Scene 3's narration offers no day-of-week anchor, which means a reader might assume Scene 3 follows directly from Scene 1. When Scene 2 is drafted, the timeline should be made explicit.

Additionally: Scene 3 references "morning air" during deployment, and symptoms manifest at "thirty-two minutes after deployment," after which Dunhilton calls and it is still daytime (Gerry has a "clear view" of the protest, Health Department vehicles approach). The scene's internal timeline is consistent.

## Geography

The occupation geography is established: **Bent Street Plaza** at the base of **TRAMP Tower**, in the central business and political district, **above the Layer** (the Layer being a weather/atmospheric boundary that separates districts socially and physically). **Potential flag:** Scene 1 describes TRAMP Tower as the location the Airborne settle at ("Bent Street Plaza at the base of TRAMP Tower"). Scene 3's laboratory is "beneath Tramp Tower." Not a firm continuity error, but worth flagging for when Scene 2 is drafted.

## Mandate

At chapter scope, the Pacing Reader evaluates scene-to-scene rhythm: how scenes breathe relative to each other, whether the chapter accelerates or stalls, and whether the chapter's emotional register escalates in a way that earns its landing.

---

## Scene 1 — Occupy Bent Street

**Length:** Substantial — the longest piece of writing in the chapter.
**Rhythm:** Accumulative. The scene builds by accretion — each paragraph adds a layer to the occupation without any single beat dominating. This is appropriate for a crowd scene and creates genuine momentum through sheer abundance of life.
**Internal pacing:** The scene has natural breathing points (the three `#` section breaks). These divisions are:
- Section 1: Night into dawn; the establishment of the occupation and its provisions
- Section 2: Sunday afternoon; T-shirts, news crews, spokesperson chaos
- Section 3: Dunhilton's address, riot squads, second night

The pacing within each section is consistent, but the **transition from Section 2 to Section 3** is the sharpest gear-change in the scene. Dunhilton's address arrives without preparation — one sentence we are watching the camera find the Elvine at the grill, the next "the word came up the feeds a little after five." This transition works, but it is the moment the scene pivots from comic to serious, and it arrives very quickly. One additional sentence of the crowd's mood before Dunhilton speaks might smooth the pivot.

**Beats-per-section ratio:** Section 1 is the longest and densest. Section 3 is the most narratively eventful (Dunhilton + riot squads + coda). Section 2 is a comic interlude that functionally delays the Dunhilton address and allows the reader to breathe after the breakfast sequence. This structure is sound.

## Scene 2 — The Monday Conversation

**Absent.** Cannot evaluate pacing.

**Pacing implication of absence:** Scene 1 ends with warmth, community, quiet singing, the forwarding-address image. Scene 3 begins with a secure laboratory, hazmat suits, and a countdown to biological attack. Without Scene 2, the chapter's pacing moves from its slowest, warmest beat directly to its coldest, fastest beat. This is a tempo problem regardless of tone — there is no intermediate scene to modulate the transition.

## Scene 3 — Operation Cleanse

**Length:** Shorter than Scene 1.
**Rhythm:** Procedural countdown. The pacing is faster than Scene 1 — scenes in secure laboratories with deployment timers run faster than crowd scenes with go-karts and yurts.
**Internal pacing:** The scene has three phases — preparation (lab), authorisation (Gerry's office, pre-deployment), and aftermath (symptoms manifest, chaos, Dunhilton calls, segregation). The preparation phase is the slowest and carries the most expository weight. The aftermath phase is the fastest and the most event-dense. This is the correct escalation curve within the scene.

**Problems:**
- The two Dunhilton phone calls interrupt the scene's momentum at the moments when the action below is most dramatic. First call arrives just as symptoms are beginning to manifest — this is the scene's most visceral moment, and the phone call pulls us back to Gerry's office to receive exposition. Second call arrives as segregation is being enforced — same problem. Both calls are pacing interruptions that serve characterisation and exposition goals at the cost of scene momentum.
- The scene ends on a **description of aftermath** rather than an action. The final image (instruments abandoned, quarantine vehicles) is static. After the procedural energy of the deployment sequence, a static close deflates the scene's drive rather than landing it.

## Chapter-level Pacing

| Scene | Tempo | Emotional register | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scene 1 | Slow to medium; accumulative | Comic → warm → ominous | Long |
| Scene 2 | UNKNOWN | UNKNOWN | UNKNOWN |
| Scene 3 | Medium to fast; procedural | Cold → horrific | Medium |

**The chapter's current pacing problem is the missing hinge.** Scene 1 ends warm and quiet. Scene 3 begins cold and procedural. A chapter that moves from communal singing and marshmallows to targeted biological attack requires a transitional scene that modulates both tempo and register. The Monday Conversation should presumably:
- Raise the stakes visible to the reader (not just Dunhilton's side)
- Shift the pacing gear between Scene 1's warmth and Scene 3's cold
- Establish something — a character, a relationship, a moment of hope or decision — that Scene 3's horror then destroys

Without Scene 2, the chapter's emotional arc is: **warmth → nothing → horror**. The gap is felt as a pacing rupture, not a dramatic choice.

---

## Chapter rhythm: macro

Scene 1 is approximately five times longer than Scene 3. This imbalance is appropriate if Scene 2 is of medium length and serves as a transitional gear-change. If Scene 2 is short, the chapter will feel back-weighted toward the horror and the warmth of Scene 1 will read as disproportionate prologue rather than the foundation it should be.

## Hard Craft Question

Scene 1 gives the occupation a full day and a full night — hundreds of named details, dozens of small comic beats — and earns an emotional investment in the community it depicts. Scene 3 destroys that community in a handful of paragraphs. **Is the disproportion in length and weight between the two scenes a deliberate structural choice** — the long, slow building of something precious before its rapid destruction — **or is Scene 3 underwritten relative to the weight it must carry?** If the former, does the reader's investment in the community built in Scene 1 actually register in Scene 3, or does the register shift prevent it from landing?
