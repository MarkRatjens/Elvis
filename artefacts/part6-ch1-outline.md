# Part 6, Chapter 1 — a working outline

*For drafting. Written 2026-08-13 from decisions the author made 11–13 August.
No numbers anywhere: where the scene wants a quantity, there is a gap marked
`[ ]` for the author to fill.*

---

## Settled, so you don't have to re-decide it

- **Chanta makes the decree**, not the High Elvis, and the chapter ends on it.
- **Nobody is persuaded of anything.** No argument in the room, no one pushing
  back. The chapter has no opposition in it. Refusal belongs to Humble later.
- **The thing is already happening.** Chanta isn't starting anything. He is
  telling people what the thing they are already doing turns out to be for.
- **Kathy has kept nothing from anybody** and knows she is not at fault. She
  comes in with pieces of a puzzle that has not formed. She has never had a
  reason to join them up, because until the sickness there was no question.
- **Why she has never seen this before:** the only Elvi she has examined are
  the dead and the dying, because those are the ones who come to a doctor.
  Nobody turns up at a clinic to report feeling wonderful. Tonight is the
  first time she has been in a room full of the ones who are well.
- **Sarah says one thing all night** and it is not addressed to anyone.
- **Chanta never crosses the room.** Once he sits on the edge of the dais he
  stays there until he stands up to speak. His drinks come to him.
- **Sarah never crosses the room either.** She is in the office chair. The
  room comes past her.
- **People have been exposed at different times**, so what shows on them is at
  different stages — one far gone, another barely started. That unevenness is
  what tells a doctor something is moving through a group rather than a
  coincidence.
- **Nobody remarks that Chanta looks well.** Anybody could see it and nobody
  does. This is never explained or pointed at.

## The shape, in order

### 1 — Before it starts

The pig on the roof with his cold cigar. A party that has been going for hours
before Sarah and the aunts arrive. Kathy wheels in the office chair, kicks the
Holy Momma's throne over and puts the chair in its place. Hayley waves off the
bourbon; Kathy drinks both. Walter takes the Great Teacher's throne with the
signed poster in his fist.

*What this has to do:* put everybody where they will stay, and put the poster
in Walter's hand early enough that it is furniture by the time it matters.

### 2 — Chanta cannot get away

He is circulating, half blinded by his own outfit. What is wrong is not that
he is among people who lost family — it is that everybody has begun looking at
him as though he is about to say something, and he has never said anything on
purpose in his life. He goes to the Elvines he knows on the theory that they
will not want anything from him. The theory fails: they want it too. His
attempt at consolation is a disaster and his offers to catch up later make it
worse.

*Must not slip:* he notices nothing. Not that the Elvines he knows are the ones
still standing, not that anybody looks well. He is oblivious the whole way
through and stays oblivious until the end.

### 3 — Sat down

The Chief of Staff calls places. Walter will not give the throne up, so Chanta
sits on the edge of the dais with the Elvisettes around him, which is as close
to Sarah as he can bear to be. One of them takes his shoes off. His drinks keep
arriving without his asking, which is why he never has to go to the bar again.

Nothing about the Elvines is said here. The remark that they are looking well
belongs to Chanta, after the ceremony, where it lands badly.

*Must not slip:* when Kathy does put the question, later, she cannot ask
whether he **knows** them or *knows* them. That phrasing means she has already
joined it up, and she hasn't. She asks it flat.

### 4 — The ceremony

Tom Wilson takes the spa bath. Continuity and dignity — he will not deviate, he
is a Clambake through and through, and every High Elvis hereafter will be one
too. He names the two threats: the sickness, and the Holy Rollers' schism. The
song. The mourning passage. The crowd chants the wrong slogan and he takes it
anyway. The baptism fails because nobody checked the plumbing, so they use a
jug of Pepsi.

*What this has to do:* be a full ceremony, warm and slightly shabby, and give
Kathy nothing. She is stuck in a chair for the whole of it, unable to get near
anyone. That is the point of it being long.

### 5 — The room afterwards

It doesn't empty, it churns. A wake and a coronation happening a few metres
apart with nobody sure which they are at. Sarah stays put; people file past to
touch the belly and to ask indiscreet questions about who the father is. The
High Elvis comes and sits with her.

Walter shoves through the throng to get his poster signed, and the High Elvis
signs it alongside the signature already on it. Walter holds it to a chandelier
and sees that the two are identical, and rolls it up without showing anybody.

Kathy goes into the crowd, and she and Hayley take the mezzanine.

*What she is actually doing:* getting sleeves pushed up at a party without
saying why. It is a practical job, not thinking.

### 6 — What she finds

From the rail, three people get out of chairs below. Two of them do it the way
everybody in that room does it — a hand on the arm of the thing, a pause, a
noise. The third just stands up, still talking. Nobody looks at any of them.

Behind them on the mezzanine, a group of Elvines are catching up who have not
seen each other in years. One of them squeals — *what happened to your scar?* —
and Maile Duval holds her arm out, almost vanished, delighted. Kathy steps into
the circle and asks if she may look, starts to say she's a doctor, and is told
they know perfectly well who she is.

She gets the flashlight out and cannot find the burn. What is there is a little
patchy redness like a skin irritation, running wrist to the inside of the elbow
and halfway to the armpit, redder at each end and paler in the middle, like a
word rubbed off a blackboard by somebody in a hurry. No ridges under the palm.
Maile has had it since she was fifteen. Twenty-five years.

It has to be a scar and not ink. Ink cannot go the way this book has already
shown marks going — Chanta's burns went overnight because scar tissue is living
tissue, and old ink is inert particles that regrowing skin leaves where they
are. No Elvi in the manuscript has a tattoo.

**Where the burn came from.** Her boyfriend's car: a pink and white Cadillac,
which she thinks he loved more than he loved her. He'd done a show out of town
and was driving her home after. About halfway it started smelling hot and she
said so, and he said it was the road — it was the brakes. Smoke got in and she
still had to beg him to stop. They stood in the grass and watched it go, and he
sat down: no shouting, no running about, no trying to save anything, just gave
up and sat with his hands hanging between his knees and tears down his face,
because every single thing he owned was in it. She went in for the guitar. The
vinyl on a guitar case goes up sudden and hot; she doesn't remember flames at
all, only the heat, and then standing six paces back with her blouse
smouldering.

**Who tells it.** Maile tells it and her friend — the one she's had since they
were fourteen — supplies the parts Maile skips over, the brakes and the
guitar, as though the ordeal had happened to her. **Hayley asks the two
questions that move it along** ("How did it happen?", "How were you burned?").
Kathy says almost nothing and keeps hold of the arm.

*This is the beat that lands.* The doctor's hand goes over a twenty-five-year-old
burn and finds no ridges, and the doctor doubts it was ever serious. Nobody in
the circle takes it as anything but good news.

*Must not slip:* Kathy does not work anything out here. She recognises
something. She has seen a body behave like this before, in blood she has never
mentioned to anyone, and there is no moment where she says so.

### 6a — The consolation, then the challenge

Chanta reaches for something consoling and lands on *"You're all looking
well."* It goes down like a dropped croquembouche — one noise, then a long
quiet, then profiteroles arriving at people's feet for the rest of the night.
He tries to get out from under it, fails, retreats into the beer and takes his
wake back towards the dais.

His line currently arrives with no approach; he is simply there.

Then Dianne Carter, who has been watching Kathy work along the arms all night,
rounds on her. She is not shouting and it is worse than shouting: she has been
going to funerals in perfect health, cousins of hers are in the ground, nobody
will tell her why, and here's Aunt Kathy going up and down the room looking at
everybody's arms. *"So go on. Is it us? Are we next?"* Kathy has never
pretended to an answer she did not have, so she takes the arm and looks at it
properly, and what she finds in the crook of it is skin like the inside of a
shell.

*Order matters here.* Dianne comes **after** the blunder, not before it. Put
her earlier and she interrupts the run from Kathy recognising it to Kathy
linking it to Chanta. Put her here and the run is unbroken, and her question —
which Kathy cannot answer — is what sends Kathy across the room after him.

*Gap to fill:* `[how many funerals Dianne has been the healthiest thing at]`.

### 6b — What forty means

At the rail, arm in arm, after Kathy has given Hayley the arithmetic: the car
fire was twenty-five years ago, Maile is forty. Hayley jokes. Kathy doesn't
laugh, and Hayley — who reads faces for a living — stops too.

Then Kathy tells her what forty is for an Elvi, and it is the measure the whole
chapter is taken against. The heart first: working against a pressure that
shouldn't be there, on arteries already narrowing. Then the gut, the kidneys,
the eyes twice over, the knees and spine, the breathing that stops in sleep. Hayley asks how much of it is Grayling, and the
answer is: the allergies, the pineapple, the series things — somebody sat down
and wrote those in — and the heart was already in the man they copied. That is
the whole division, and it is all the scene claims. They have bred inside the
series ever since, from a man who was the grandson of first cousins: the most
inbred population in Ulyssia.

*(An earlier version of this beat had Grayling's allergy as "the lesser clock…
the one you can be angry at because it has a name." Both halves are gone: the
epigram was cut in drafting as invented attitude ([[working-principles]] §21),
and "clock" language is ruled out entirely — the Grayling clock is Elvi
apocrypha, not design. See [[world-rules/biology]] → "The Grayling clock is
apocrypha." Kathy does not editorialise here; she divides and stops.)*

Half her practice is telling an Elvis in his prime he won't see fifty. The
other half is burying the ones who didn't believe her. There isn't a third
half.

Then the tell she uses without meaning to — how they get out of chairs — which
pays off the three who got out of chairs at the top of §6. It ends on the fact
and not the conclusion: *Maile is forty, and I have just had her arm in my
hands, and there is nothing wrong with it.*

*They inherit the ailments, never the biography.* The real Presley's decline
was substantially prescribed to him; the Elvi's is not, and Kathy says so. Full
clinical detail in `world-rules/biology` → "What an Elvi is at forty"; source
material in `research/elvis-ageing-projection`.

*Gap to fill:* `[how many Elvi Kathy has buried]` — she knows all their names.

### 7 — Said out loud, near enough for Chanta to hear

**The two of them work as a pair, and the split is fixed:** Hayley asks the
questions, Kathy reads the bodies. Hayley is a detective on her night off who
never gets off duty; Kathy is the doctor. Neither does the other's job.

Kathy cannot show her working, because it starts with blood she took from Sarah
while Sarah was asleep. So she never goes near it. She sits down on the dais
beside him, which nobody does, and which puts her at the height of his ear.
Hayley stays standing.

Hayley asks whether he knows the Elvines by the curtains, then how many of them
he has slept with, then how long they have been with him. He answers all three
without once asking himself why he is being asked, because a corner of him has
spent his whole life waiting for somebody in authority to ask him a question he
could actually answer. He does ask *why* — and nobody tells him. Then Hayley
says: then you're carrying something. He hears a venereal accusation and
defends himself at a volume that carries twice as far as he means it to.

Kathy takes the beer out of his hand and turns him round to look at them.
Betty Lee's roots have gone back to their own colour. Marguerita's knee is
better. Dianne Carter was on the plaza, has been to more funerals than Kathy
has, and has the arms of a girl. And Chanta himself has got steadily better
since they brought him in, in a way nobody in the room has once remarked on,
including him.

She reaches for the right word — **salutogenic** — and it lands on nobody, so
she has to say it again in plain language. The plain version is the one Chanta
picks up.

Sarah, without turning round: *"Then it can be moved."* No weight on it. Not to
anyone.

*Gaps to fill:* `[how long Marguerita has been complaining about the knee]`,
`[how long the Elvines have been with him]`.

### 8 — He gets there

Not by following the reasoning — he can't. He arrives at the right answer by a
route nobody else can retrace, and he is certain about it in a way that is not
supported by anything he understands. Kathy tries to slow him down. He tells
her she has been saying it wrong.

*Must not slip:* he does not count anything, and he does not become clever. He
is the same man he was two pages ago, who has simply landed on the truth.

### 9 — The decree

He takes the spa bath in the cape and the crown and does not use the
ceremonial voice.

The room goes quiet the way it went quiet once before, for a man on that same
bath who asked them to sweep the streets — and that is the only weight the
moment needs.

What he tells them is **news, not permission**. Nothing is forbidden and
nothing is being allowed. He tells them the thing that saves you is already
here, some of you already have it and don't know, and this is how it travels.
Then he says what it is, and says it again in the plainest word available.

**"Go forth and hump."**

The chapter ends. Nothing after it. No reaction from the High Elvis.

- **Sarah is in two seats.** Kathy wheels the office chair in and kicks the
  Holy Momma's throne over to make room for it — then after the ceremony Sarah
  finds "the comfiest couch in the room and flumped into it," and the High
  Elvis joins her there. But the decree scene has her speaking "from the
  chair," and *she never crosses the room* is settled. One of the two seats has
  to go.

  Dianne Carter up on the mezzanine, and Chanta's "you're all looking well"
  lands on a group "down there," watched from above. But from the dais Kathy
  points at "that group by the curtains" and "Dianne Carter over there" as
  though they were the people she has just had by the wrist. Either the
  examination comes down to the floor, or the curtains group goes up.
