# Suggested front-matter disclaimer

Companion to [inventory.md](inventory.md). Not legal advice — a drafting aid. The disclaimer is not a defence in itself; it does no legal work on the copyright question and only modest work on the rest. What it *does* do is address the one prong a court actually weighs against a title like this — whether the work is "explicitly misleading" about sponsorship (*Rogers v. Grimaldi*) — and set the framing any publisher's counsel reads first. Cheap insurance, worth having, worth getting right.

---

## The page (copyright-page verso, or its own leaf)

> *Clones of Elvis* is a work of satire. Names, characters, businesses, places, events, products, and incidents are either the product of the author's imagination or are used fictitiously and satirically. Any resemblance to actual persons, living or dead, or to actual companies, products, or events, is intended as parody and social comment, or is entirely coincidental.
>
> This book is an independent work of fiction. It is not authorised, endorsed, licensed, or sponsored by, and has no affiliation with, the Estate of Elvis Presley, Elvis Presley Enterprises, Inc., ABG EPE IP LLC, or any person or entity holding rights in the name, likeness, recordings, or musical compositions of Elvis Presley. All trademarks are the property of their respective owners and are referred to for the purposes of commentary, criticism, parody, and satire.
>
> Song titles and lyrics have been deliberately altered throughout. Where a phrase evokes an existing song, the evocation is parodic and transformative, and no reproduction of any copyrighted lyric is intended.

## Notes on each clause (for future-Mark)

**Clause 1 — the standard fiction disclaimer, with a twist.** The stock wording is "any resemblance … is entirely coincidental." That line is a lie in your case and a lie is worse than no clause: the resemblances to Elvis, Dawkins, and Dutton are the point. So the clause is amended to *"intended as parody and social comment, or is entirely coincidental"* — which is both true and does the affirmative work of putting the parody characterisation on the record from page one.

**Clause 2 — the non-affiliation clause.** This is the one that earns its place. It speaks directly to the *Rogers/Capece* sponsorship question: the danger in the Elvis cases was never the book, it was the implication of estate blessing. Naming EPE (and ABG EPE IP LLC, the Authentic Brands vehicle that now holds the rights) and disclaiming endorsement removes the "explicitly misleading" hook before anyone reaches for it. The "trademarks … for the purposes of commentary, criticism, parody, and satire" sentence pre-frames "Graceland" and "Memphis Mafia."

**Clause 3 — the lyrics clause.** Documents intent for the mondegreen method. Once the Inauguration medley is rewritten (see [inventory.md](inventory.md) §1), this clause is true across the whole manuscript and worth keeping as belt-and-braces. If any verbatim lyric line survives a redraft, this clause is doing more work than it should and the line should go instead.

## Placement and posture

- **Copyright-page verso** is the conventional home, in small type. A satirical novel can also afford to put a lighter-touch version on its own leaf as part of the comic apparatus — but keep a straight version somewhere; the joke and the shield are different jobs.
- **The disclaimer protects the pages, not the cover.** Per [inventory.md](inventory.md) §5, the real publicity-rights exposure is in marketing and cover art. No disclaimer offsets a cover that uses Elvis's actual likeness or EPE's marks (TCB, the lightning bolt). Cover discipline is the separate, higher-scrutiny decision.
- **Verify the rights-holder names before print.** The Presley rights have moved (Authentic Brands Group / ABG EPE IP LLC control the commercial rights; Elvis Presley Enterprises remains the operating entity for Graceland). Confirm the correct current legal names with counsel at the point of publication rather than trusting this draft.
