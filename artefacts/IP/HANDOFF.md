## LOOSE ENDS — what the author must pick up

Ordered by urgency. Items 1–2 are the only *manuscript* changes the risk analysis actually requires; the rest are decisions and process.

1. **Rewrite the Inauguration medley.** Chapter "The Inauguration of The High Elvis" (~line 2299 of `ClonesOfElvis.docx`). This is the single highest-risk passage in the book: the High Elvis's speech strings together short *verbatim* fragments of ~15 Elvis songs as prose. Individually the title-length fragments ("all shook up," "burning love") aren't copyrightable and can stay; the exposure is in the **line-length lyric fragments**, above all **"my hands are shaking and my knees are weak"** (← *All Shook Up*). Mondegreen-ise those the way the rest of the book already works. *Status: agreed, not yet drafted. Author was happy to do this / wanted more mondegreens. Claude offered to draft a version — not taken up.*

2. **Review the minor verbatim echoes.** Lower stakes, quick calls: "caught in a trap" (narration, ~line 5372, *Suspicious Minds*); "Are you lonesome tonight?" as a voicemail greeting (~5757); "I have a burning love for all of you in the Ghetto" (~5499). Already-safe because parodic: "We can't go on together, with religious minds" (~5843, one-line rewrite). Decide per line whether to bend or leave.

3. **Choose the book title.** "Clones of Elvis" is a working title only; a short mondegreen is the agreed strategy. Candidates gathered below and in [titles & likenesses.md](titles%20&%20likenesses.md). Decision pending. Note the subtitle option to protect discoverability.

4. **Cover art — highest-scrutiny decision in the project.** The manuscript is well protected; the *cover and marketing* are where publicity/trademark risk actually bites. Rules in [titles & likenesses.md](titles%20&%20likenesses.md): favour original illustration over photo; favour *multiplicity* (a production line of Elvi) over a single loving portrait; keep the TCB lightning-bolt logo and the distinctive Aloha-eagle jumpsuit *off*; nothing implying estate endorsement. If a photo of a real impersonator is used, a **model release + photographer's licence** are required. There is an `elvis-icon.png`/`.svg` in the project root — treat any such asset as a cover-scrutiny item, not a freebie.

5. **Add the disclaimer page to the front matter.** It does not currently exist in the compiled manuscript. Draft ready in [disclaimer.md](disclaimer.md). Note the deliberate amendment to the stock "any resemblance is coincidental" line (it would be a false statement here) → "intended as parody and social comment, or is entirely coincidental."

6. **Verify the rights-holder legal names at print time.** The Presley rights have moved to Authentic Brands Group; the current holders are **ABG EPE IP LLC** and **Elvis Presley Enterprises, Inc.** Confirm exact current legal names with counsel before the disclaimer goes to print.

7. **Decide consciously on Richard Dawkins.** He is a real, living person and the novel's central *deified* character ("the Dawkins," born as "Little Richard"). Portrayal is admiring, he's a public figure, and *de Havilland* protects fictional portrayals of real people — so risk is low — but he is the one living person with any conceivable claim. Make it a conscious decision that you're comfortable with him reading it.

8. **Keep the Dutton resemblance deniable.** The villain Piotr Dunhilton is a documented lampoon of Peter Dutton (per your working notes; the real name is not in the text). Dutton has actually sued for defamation and won (2021), and Australian defamation law is plaintiff-friendly. Your protections hold (different name, fantastical setting, comic opinion, political satire is core protected speech) — but this is the one place "keep it deniable" is a live drafting principle rather than a formality. Don't let the name or any biographical tell drift closer.

9. **Get a pre-publication legal review.** Everything here is orientation, not advice. A publishing/IP lawyer should sign off before release — especially the medley (#1) and the cover (#4).

**Low-risk, logged, no action needed:** the *Gremlins* warranty parody (Mogwai-rules pastiche), the *Far Side*/Larson meta-joke (self-aware about infringement), the Muppets cameo, the Hitchhiker's/Douglas Adams allusions. All parody/allusion, none reproduces protected expression at length.

## The defence, in one page

**Overall posture is strong for what the book is.** The method is already remarkably defensible: essentially no sustained verbatim lyric anywhere; every chapter title is a mondegreen; every sung lyric is a parody rewrite or gets interrupted before it completes; and the working notes document this as deliberate method — which is exactly the evidence of transformative intent a defence wants.

**The author's four stated defences, tested:**

1. *"It's a satire."* Cuts both ways by jurisdiction. Under **US** fair use (*Campbell v. Acuff-Rose*), satire is *weaker* than parody: parody must borrow its target to make its point; satire "can stand on its own two feet and so requires justification for the very act of borrowing." If the target is capitalism rather than the songs, you had *less* need to borrow the songs. Under **Australian** law it's the opposite — s 41A of the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) expressly protects fair dealing for "parody **or satire**." So this defence is strong at home, mediocre in the US market; international publishers assess to the US standard.

2. *"The clones reflect on impersonators, not the man."* Reframe this — the Elvi are literal biological clones, not impersonators, so as stated it doesn't hold. The **stronger** version, which is your best single argument: the book is *commentary on the Elvis industry itself* — the real-world practice of turning a dead man into IP, which is exactly what EPE does. That converts much of the use from satire into **parody-of-the-phenomenon**: you cannot satirise the commodification of Elvis without using Elvis. Lead with this.

3. *"The piece genuinely respects Elvis."* True (the Epilogue is affectionate and exculpatory — blames the exploiters, not the man). Legally it buys little on copyright and nothing on defamation (the dead can't be defamed in the US or Australia), but it reinforces the commentary framing and helps publisher/estate temperature. Note: no living Presley family member appears in the book, removing that class of claimant.

4. *"The real target is Latter Day Capitalism."* Helps under Australian s 41A and on the publicity side (makes the work more clearly expressive commentary); hurts on US lyric fair use (see #1). Keep it, subordinate to the industry-commentary framing.

**Right of publicity** — the thing people fear most with Elvis — mostly doesn't reach a novel. The estate's postmortem rights are famously strong (Tennessee's 1984 statute, renewed by the 2024 ELVIS Act), but the case law protects expressive works: *de Havilland v. FX* holds a celebrity "does not own history" and can't veto a creator's portrayal in fact or fiction, and the ELVIS Act carries express exemptions for comment, criticism, satire, and parody. Publicity claims win against *merchandise, advertising, endorsement* — not against books as books. Australia has no general right of publicity at all. **Where it bites is outside the covers** (see loose end #4): in *EPE v. Capece* the parody defence failed largely because the *advertising* used Elvis's name and image. The title "Clones of Elvis" is fine under *Rogers v. Grimaldi* (artistically relevant, not explicitly misleading); the cover and promotion are the exposure.

**Song lyrics in fiction — practical reality.** Music publishers are notoriously litigious over even a single quoted line; publishers routinely demand permission regardless of fair-use merits because defending costs more than cutting the line. This is why loose end #1 matters even though the fragments are individually short — it's the *density* of recognisable hooks in one passage that draws fire.

## Title candidates (corrected method)

The mondegreen discipline: preserve **syllable count, stress pattern, and stressed vowels at multiple positions across the line** so the original song is audible throughout — not just a terminal rhyme on a skeleton. Model: "You ain't **noth**in' but a **hound dog**" → "Your Aunt **Buff**ing up a **Ground**hog" (three distributed rhymes: hound/Ground /aʊnd/, dog/hog /ɒɡ/, noth/Buff /ʌ/). Avoid the one-word-swap cheat (keep everything, change the last word).

Best candidates, by theme:

- **Clonesome Tonight** ← *Are You Lonesome Tonight* — clone/lonesome portmanteau; carries premise + Epilogue melancholy; short and sayable.
- **Grayling for Keeps** ← *Playing for Keeps* — gets the corporation's name in: Play→**Gray** holds /eɪ/, both stop+liquid onsets (pl-/gr-), "for Keeps" untouched; means the corp *keeps* (owns) you. Caveat: lesser-known song, may read as the idiom. (Bonus: "**Grayling**" already shadows "**Graceland**" — /ɡreɪ-/ + -l- pivot.)
- **A Little Less Compensation** ← *A Little Less Conversation* — near-minimal pair; wages/exploitation.
- **New Slave Crews** ← *Blue Suede Shoes* — three-for-three stressed vowels (uː/eɪ/uːz); the "reinvented slavery" thread.
- **Sale House Stock** ← *Jailhouse Rock* — jail→Sale /eɪl/, rock→Stock /ɒk/; the Elvi as inventory. (Also **Grayling Stock** for the corp name off a famous song, weaker middle.)
- **Return to Vendor** / **Re-earn the Tender** ← *Return to Sender* — commodification / legal-tender capitalism.
- **In the Debt, Though** ← *In the Ghetto* — debt-bondage, keeps the melancholy.
- **Clone Me, Vendor** ← *Love Me Tender* — Vendor/Tender is a full rhyme (tighter than the earlier "Clone My Waiter"; trades the servitude image for phonetic tightness).

**Interior device, not the title:** "Clone My Waiter," "Clone My Bellhop," "Clone My Porter" — run these as recurring chapter/part headings naming each Elvi's servitude role. Better as a running joke about the product line than as the spine of the book.

**Subtitle strategy:** a mondegreen main title drops the word "Elvis" (costs discoverability, marginally helps IP posture). Recover it with a clarifying subtitle — *Clonesome Tonight: [a satire]* — or hold "Clones of Elvis" as the subtitle under a punchier head.

## Key authorities (for re-grounding in another chat)

- *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994) — parody vs satire under US fair use.
- Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) **s 41A** — Australian fair dealing for parody *or* satire.
- *de Havilland v. FX Networks* (Cal. Ct. App. 2018) — First Amendment protects fictional portrayal of real people; "does not own history."
- *Midler v. Ford* (9th Cir. 1988) / *White v. Samsung* (9th Cir. 1992) — right of publicity protects *evoked* identity (impersonator/look-alike), in *advertising*.
- *Rogers v. Grimaldi* (2d Cir. 1989) — celebrity name/image in an expressive work's title/content: artistic-relevance + not-explicitly-misleading test.
- *EPE v. Capece*, 141 F.3d 188 (5th Cir. 1998) — parody defence failed on the *advertising*, not the concept.
- Tennessee ELVIS Act (2024) — renewed postmortem publicity rights; exemptions for comment/criticism/satire/parody.
