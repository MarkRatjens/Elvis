How close can you sail with an impersonator likeness?
The uncomfortable first fact: using an impersonator instead of Elvis does not give you the clean pass the framing implies. The right of publicity protects identity, and the case law is explicit that identity can be evoked without the literal name or face. In Midler v. Ford a sound-alike singer triggered liability; in White v. Samsung a robot in a wig and gown gesturing at a game-show wheel evoked Vanna White enough to be actionable. The courts' phrasing is that defendants "cannot avoid liability by cleverly sidestepping the use of a celebrity's literal name or face while still exploiting her persona." An Elvis impersonator's entire function is to evoke Elvis — so as a matter of publicity law, an impersonator on the cover is the evocation, not an escape from it.

But — and this is where you actually have room — Midler and White were advertising cases: commercial speech selling cars and VCRs. Your cover is part of an expressive work that is literally about Elvis cloning and impersonation, which puts you under Rogers v. Grimaldi and de Havilland, where the imagery only has to be artistically relevant to the work and not explicitly mislead about endorsement. A cover depicting an Elvis-like figure on a book called [something] about mass-produced Elvi is about as artistically relevant as an image can get. So the real question isn't "will I lose a lawsuit" — you probably wouldn't — it's "will the estate send a cease-and-desist, and will my publisher blink," and on that axis the details matter a lot.

So, ranked from safest to most exposed:

An original illustration that evokes the archetype — a stylised jumpsuit-and-quiff figure, or better, many of them — is the safest berth. It sidesteps two problems at once: it doesn't reproduce Elvis's actual face (weaker publicity evocation of the man), and it avoids the second rights-holder you'd otherwise acquire — the real impersonator, who has his own publicity rights and whom you'd need a signed model release from, plus the photographer. Illustration also sits most naturally in the satirical/sci-fi register.
A photograph of a real, hired impersonator is workable but drags in that model release and photographer's licence, and a photo reads more as "the real Elvis" than a drawing does, nudging you toward the merchandise end.
A photorealistic, lovingly rendered single Elvis face is the most exposed option — it reads least like commentary and most like a licensed Elvis product, which is exactly the merchandise use publicity law is for.
Two hard lines regardless of route. First, keep the specific trademarked elements off it — the TCB lightning-bolt logo, and the exact distinctive jumpsuit designs the estate treats as protected trade dress; a generic rhinestone jumpsuit is fine, the Aloha eagle suit is a flag. Second, nothing that implies endorsement or authorised-biography status. And the strategic point that ties back to your own book: a cover foregrounding multiplicity — a production line of Elvi, a vat, a barcode, clones stretching to the horizon — is simultaneously more expressive (stronger Rogers relatedness, obviously satirical) and less like a straight Elvis portrait (weaker merchandise read). The concept protects you. The loving single portrait is the one place the impersonator framing doesn't help, because the whole point of a good impersonator is that he is the evocation.

## Corrected mondegreen method

The discipline the first list missed: preserve **syllable count, stress pattern, and the stressed vowels at multiple positions across the line** — so the original song is audible the whole way through, not just at a terminal rhyme. Model: "You ain't **noth**in' but a **hound dog**" → "Your Aunt **Buff**ing up a **Ground**hog" — three distributed rhymes (hound/Ground /aʊnd/, dog/hog /ɒɡ/, noth/Buff /ʌ/), not one. Reject the one-word-swap cheat (keep everything, change the last word — e.g. "Viva Las Wages," "Seditious Minds").
