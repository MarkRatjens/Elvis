Bottom line up front: there is essentially no clean verbatim reproduction of a full Elvis lyric. The book's entire method is mondegreen and parody — altered song titles as chapter headings, and deliberately rewritten ("evolved") lyrics. The one real exposure is a single dense paragraph that strings together short verbatim fragments of many Elvis songs (category 1 below). The larger right-of-publicity questions attach to the pervasive use of the Elvis persona itself and to two named real people (Richard Dawkins, and — in author notes only — Peter Dutton).

1. Song lyrics quoted verbatim — LOW volume, one concentrated instance
There is no passage that reproduces a sustained run of a copyrighted lyric verbatim. The Elvi are repeatedly shown unable to agree on lyrics and fighting over them, and every time a song is actually "sung" in the text it is a rewritten parody, not the real words. Two authorial devices deliberately avoid quotation:

The narrator interrupts before any real lyric completes. E.g. scene "Blue Shade Blues," an Elvi sings only "My blue suede shoes / Were made by—" before another Elvi shoots the TV; then "Don't step on my—" / "Shut up!" (lines 4531–4536). The scene ends with "Biffing over song lyrics?" — the interruption is the joke.
Parody rewrites to the tune of real songs. E.g. "Love Me Tender" becomes "Evolve me tender, / Evolve me true. / Evolve me at your will…" and "Evolve me tender, / Evolve me strong…" (lines 2183–2208); an interrupted "Don't Be Cruel"–style verse "Loathe my gender / Loathe my beard…" (lines 2165–2169).
The one real concern — a verbatim mini-medley. In the chapter "The Inauguration of The High Elvis" (line 2299), the High Elvis's speech is a single paragraph woven almost entirely from short, recognizable, verbatim Elvis lyric/title fragments run together as prose:

"I am all shook up… my hands are shaking and my knees are weak… such a fool as I… this pocket full of rainbows. I have a burning love for all of you in the Ghetto. Suspicious minds may think I have taken this role to be the big boss man, but you are always on my mind… you'll never walk alone. What we need is a little less conversation and a little more action… I washed my hands in muddy waters and they came out clean. It is no secret… only the strong survive so please don't stop loving me."

That single sentence lifts identifiable phrases from at least ~15 songs (All Shook Up; A Fool Such As I; Follow That Dream / "pocketful of rainbows"; Burning Love; In the Ghetto; Suspicious Minds; Big Boss Man; Always on My Mind; You'll Never Walk Alone; A Little Less Conversation; Only the Strong Survive; Please Don't Stop Loving Me; Peace in the Valley; etc.). Each fragment is short (2–6 words), but there are many, densely packed. This is the highest-risk single passage in the book and should be flagged. A shorter echo appears at line 5499 ("I have a burning love for all of you in the Ghetto").

Other isolated verbatim short phrases used as prose, not sung lyric:

"caught in a trap" (Suspicious Minds) — narration: "Heston felt caught in a trap" (line 5372).
"Are you lonesome tonight?" — used as the High Elvis's voicemail greeting (line 5757).
"We can't go on together, with religious minds" (line 5843) — a one-line rewrite of Suspicious Minds' "we can't go on together / with suspicious minds" (parody, one line).
No verbatim lyrics of non-Elvis songs were found. Johnny Cash's "Ring of Fire" appears only as a simile ("exploded like a burning ring of fire," line 5851) — idea/phrase, not a lyric quote.

3. Elvis's name / persona — CENTRAL, and the depiction is two-faced (mocking body + sympathetic thesis)
The historical Elvis Presley is named (line 865, a multiverse gag: "eclipsing even Elvis Presley who exists in 97.68%" of universes). The "real Elvis" is discussed as a prophesied figure (lines 2603–2619: "the real Elvis isn't Estonian," "the Dawkins loved so much as a boy").
Real Elvis biography is mined for comedy throughout: the Nixon narcotics-badge meeting is dramatized ("a Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs badge, identical to the one Richard Nixon gave Elvis in 1970," line 914; a "Narc Elvis" character); Dr. Nick / Dr. Nichopoulos → "Dr Nick" and "Dr Nick's Mix" (drug supplier, lines 1425–1455); the "418 cubes of sugar," peanut butter, bacon fat, prescription-drug, jumpsuit/rhinestone/karate/"Thank you very much" iconography.
The Epilogue (final chapter, "Elvis," line 5858) is the book's sympathetic thesis about the real man. It imagines a counterfactual universe where Elvis lived well — an explicit indictment-by-contrast of how the real Elvis was exploited: "He never suffered from the genetic lottery that brewed his faulty, in-bred DNA; he grew up in dignity… he was never exploited by avaricious management; there were no Catholics to report him to the FBI director… souvenir-hungry mobs never stripped him naked… he never felt the need to recluse to Graceland for the sake of safety; he received and took good medical advice; and he was never conscripted… to a war. This Elvis lived… into his nineties…" The mockery of the body (a slapstick death: he "threw his hip so far, it cracked," then accidentally hanged himself on his gold chains mid-performance, dying with "Thank you very much" in his throat) is paired with the sincere claim that "Any Elvis of any universe deserved whatever (consensual) love and adoration came their way" and that this un-exploited Elvis "was the loveliest. And the happiest" (line 5859). The final punchline: this happy Elvis was Estonian, named "Elvei Aaron Preestriheinamaa" (lines 5860–5863). Net stance: satirical toward Elvis's persona/appetites, but ultimately protective/exculpatory — the target is the exploiters, not the man.
4. The "clones" concept — literal biological clones (an in-world commercial product)
The Elvi are literal manufactured clones of Elvis Presley, mass-produced and sold as service androids/biological units, not impersonators. Key mechanics:

Produced/sold by the fictional "Grayling Corporation" (founder "Linus Grayling") as "ELVIS" units — an in-world backronym: "Entertainment Lifestyle Vocationally Integrated Servant" (line 1866; also "ELVIS Entertainment and Hospitality Unit," line 908). Sold "into hospitality and domestic servitude" — the text explicitly frames this as "reinvented slavery" (line 948) followed by an "emancipation of the Elvi."
They come in "clone series" named after Elvis eras/films: "GI Blues series," "King Creole Series," etc. (lines 2011, 5629). Individual Elvi carry mock-military/place names (Tulsa McLean, Major Memphis, Colonel Kalamazoo, "Suspicious Mick," Denver Elvis).
Parody warranty cards (Gremlins pastiche): void conditions include "Feeding the ELVIS after midnight," feeding it "bacon fat and coffee grounds," letting it choke on rhinestones, and — the plot engine — "Exposing the ELVIS to religion… or gospel music of any kind" (lines 1444, 1867–1869). "Never get an ELVIS angry" (line 910).
Real Elvis impersonators are explicitly distinguished from the clones: an "Elvi impersonator" performing "A Little Less Conversation" is booed and ejected by "the genuine Elvi — the real ELVIS units" (line 763). Here's the grounding, with the usual caveat that this is analysis for orientation, not legal advice.

The headline
Your overall position is stronger than your four points suggest, but two of the four don't do the work you think they do, and there is exactly one passage in the manuscript that carries real, concentrated risk. The good news first: the book's method is already remarkably defensible. There is essentially no sustained verbatim reproduction of any Elvis lyric anywhere in the draft. Every chapter title is a mondegreen, every sung lyric is either a parody rewrite ("Evolve me tender, / Evolve me true") or gets interrupted before it completes — and your own working notes document that this was systematic method, not accident, which is precisely the kind of evidence of transformative intent a defence wants.

Your four defences, tested
1) "It's a satire." Here you need to know about a distinction that cuts against you in the US and for you in Australia. In Campbell v. Acuff-Rose (the 2 Live Crew "Pretty Woman" case), the US Supreme Court drew a line between parody and satire: parody must borrow from its target to make its point, so the borrowing is justified; satire "can stand on its own two feet and so requires justification for the very act of borrowing." If your target is Latter Day Capitalism rather than Elvis's songs, then under US fair use doctrine you had less need to borrow the songs, not more — the satire label weakens a lyrics defence rather than strengthening it. Australia is the mirror image: section 41A of the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) explicitly protects fair dealing for the purpose of "parody or satire" — Parliament deliberately included satire in 2006, which US law never has. So "it's a satire" is a genuinely good answer at home and a mediocre one in the US market, and any international publisher will assess to the US standard.

2) "The clones reflect on impersonators, not the man." The inventory doesn't quite support this as stated — the Elvi are literal biological clones of Elvis Presley, manufactured and sold as ELVIS units, and the one actual impersonator in the book gets booed off stage by them. But abandon this framing, because you have a much stronger one sitting in your own text. The book's central conceit is Elvis as manufactured commercial product — warrantied, mass-produced in era-named series, sold into servitude, then emancipated, with "Tax the Tom Parkers!" as the revolutionary chant and "avaricious management" named in the Epilogue as what destroyed the real man. That means the book isn't satire that merely borrows Elvis to attack capitalism; it's commentary on the Elvis industry itself — the literal, real-world practice of turning a dead man into intellectual property, which is what Elvis Presley Enterprises does for a living. Under Campbell's logic that converts much of your use from satire into parody-of-the-phenomenon: you cannot satirise the commodification of Elvis without using Elvis. This is your strongest single argument and I'd suggest it become your first defence, not an unstated one.

3) "The piece genuinely respects Elvis." True on the evidence — the Epilogue is affectionate and explicitly exculpatory, blaming the exploiters rather than the man — but understand what this does and doesn't buy you. Legally it buys you almost nothing on copyright, and defamation is a non-issue anyway: Elvis died in 1977, and neither US nor Australian law lets the dead be defamed. Where it matters is practically — publisher comfort, estate temperature, and the rhetorical framing of any dispute — and in reinforcing point 2, since a work whose ultimate stance is "the real Elvis deserved better than the machine that consumed him" reads as commentary, not free-riding. Also worth noting from the inventory: no living Presley family member (Priscilla, Riley Keough) appears anywhere in the draft, which removes the class of claimant who could sue over portrayal.

4) "The real target is Latter Day Capitalism." As above — this helps under Australian s 41A, helps on the right-of-publicity side (it makes the work more obviously expressive commentary), and hurts on US lyric fair use. Keep it, but subordinate it to the Elvis-industry framing.

The right of publicity question — the one people worry about most with Elvis, and mostly needn't here
The Elvis estate has famously strong postmortem publicity rights — Tennessee's 1984 statute was written substantially for Elvis, and the 2024 ELVIS Act renewed it — and EPE's litigiousness is real. But the case law is consistently on the side of expressive works. In de Havilland v. FX (2018), the California Court of Appeal held that a celebrity "does not own history" and cannot "control, dictate, approve, disapprove, or veto the creator's portrayal" in a work of fact, fiction, or both; and the ELVIS Act itself carries express exemptions for works of comment, criticism, satire, and parody. A novel is the paradigm protected expressive work — publicity-rights claims win against merchandise, advertising, and endorsement implications, not against books as books. Australia, meanwhile, has no general right of publicity at all; the nearest analogues (passing off, s 18 Australian Consumer Law) require a misrepresentation of endorsement, which a satirical novel doesn't make.

The place this right actually bites is outside the covers. In Elvis Presley Enterprises v. Capece (the "Velvet Elvis" bar case), the parody defence failed at the Fifth Circuit largely because the advertising used Elvis's name and image as a draw. The lesson for you: the manuscript is well protected; the title, cover, and marketing are where care is needed. "Clones of Elvis" as a title should be fine under Rogers v. Grimaldi — it's artistically relevant to the content and not explicitly misleading about sponsorship — but the cover and promotion must never use Elvis's actual likeness, EPE's marks (TCB, the lightning bolt), or anything implying estate blessing. I notice there's an elvis-icon.png/.svg sitting in the project directory; whatever it is, treat cover art as the highest-scrutiny decision in the whole project. A standard front-matter disclaimer ("a work of satire; not affiliated with or endorsed by the estate of Elvis Presley...") doesn't exist in the compiled draft yet and is cheap insurance — not a defence in itself, but it directly addresses the "explicitly misleading" prong.

The one passage that needs an amendment decision
Chapter "The Inauguration of The High Elvis": the High Elvis's speech is a single paragraph woven from verbatim fragments of roughly fifteen Elvis songs — "all shook up... my hands are shaking and my knees are weak... burning love... in the Ghetto... suspicious minds... always on my mind... a little less conversation and a little more action... I washed my hands in muddy waters..." This is the highest-risk passage in the book, for a specific reason: individually, short phrases and titles aren't copyrightable (you can say "all shook up" or "burning love" freely — many of these fragments are just titles), but the dense aggregation of recognisable lyric hooks is exactly what music publishers pursue, they are notoriously litigious over even single lines in fiction, and this is the passage where your satire-not-parody problem is live. The practical reality reported across publishing is that publishers demand permission for even one quoted line regardless of fair-use merits, because defending the suit costs more than losing the passage.

The amendment path is cheap and already in your toolkit: the exposure concentrates in the few fragments that are actual lyric lines rather than titles — "my hands are shaking and my knees are weak" (All Shook Up) is the clearest one. Mondegreen or paraphrase the line-length fragments the way you've mondegreened everything else, keep the title-length ones verbatim, and the passage drops to the same low-risk tier as the rest of the book while keeping the joke. A distant echo of the same issue: "caught in a trap" in narration and the "We can't go on together, with religious minds" one-liner — the latter is a one-line parody rewrite and much safer; the former is four words and borderline-trivial.

Two named real people worth a thought
Richard Dawkins is the novel's deity — named, central, and living. The portrayal is admiring, he's a public figure, and de Havilland protects fictional portrayals of real people, so risk is low; but he is the one living person with any conceivable claim, and it's worth a conscious decision that you're comfortable with the portrayal being read by him. Peter Dutton never appears by name — Dunhilton is a documented lampoon in your private notes only — but be aware that Dutton has actually sued for defamation and won (the 2021 refugee-advocate tweet case), and Australian defamation law is plaintiff-friendly. Your protections are that the name differs, the character is fictional in a fantastical world, the imputation (humourlessness in a politician) is comic opinion, and political satire is close to the core of protected speech in Australia — but of everything in the book, this is the one item where "keep the resemblance deniable" is a live drafting principle rather than a formality.

Where this leaves you
Reordered for strength, your defence is: (1) the work is commentary on the commodification of Elvis itself, so the borrowing targets what it borrows — parody in the Campbell sense, not just satire; (2) the method is systematically transformative — mondegreens, rewrites, interruptions — and documented as such; (3) in Australia, s 41A protects satire explicitly; (4) publicity-rights law protects novels and the Epilogue's affection reinforces the commentary framing; (5) the residual risk is surgical, not structural — one lyric-medley paragraph, cover/marketing discipline, and a missing disclaimer page. Fix those three and I'd rate the manuscript's legal posture as genuinely strong for what it is.
