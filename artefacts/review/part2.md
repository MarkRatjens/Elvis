# Part 2 — Developmental Review

Date: 2026-03-06 | Chapters 13–22 (10 chapters, 20 scenes) | Surveyed, not developmentally edited

---

## The Shape

Ten chapters, twenty scenes. Part 2 opens by pulling the camera away from Sarah and Tony entirely — dropping the reader into a Cloudborne apartment block where a Fridge Force field agent named Chip is knocking on someone's door about a smell. It's a bold structural choice, and it pays off twice: first as misdirection (what does a stasis anomaly have to do with anything?), and then as connection, because Unit 15 next door is the Teh family — grieving a husband and father who "inexplicably committed suicide." The Gyges Disc's chain of custody just got longer, and the reader doesn't even know it yet.

From there, the Part fans outward — Alexander arrests Tony, Halo murders a Commissioner and three more victims, Sarah discovers she's pregnant, the Elvi mount a full military rescue — then snaps together in its final chapters with a triple punch: Eloise Shakti arrives in person and takes the disc at gunpoint, Sarah and Tony reunite, and the DNA test reveals the baby is a clone of Clinton Richard Dawkins. The Part closes with its most surprising grace note: Tony, minutes from eviction by the Elvi, spontaneously writes "Evolve Me Tender" and transforms from fugitive cleaner into the Elvi's songwriter. That's four tonal registers — thriller, romance, science fiction, and musical comedy — in two chapters.

## What Part 2 Does Right

**Chip's opening chapter is a masterstroke of misdirection.** The reader has no idea why they're watching a Stasis Anomalies Investigator from Fridge Force haul his industrial carcass up two flights of stairs to knock on the Chuglinghams' door about a smell. Chip is a field agent, not a lab tech — he visits homes, arrests homemakers, and scratches his plumber's crack while doing it. The Pratchettian comedy of his methodology — the way he treats what may be industrial sabotage like a plumbing inconvenience — sets a tonal benchmark for the novel's relationship with its own mythology. This is how the book does science fiction: through characters who are annoyed by it. And the adjacent Teh family in Unit 15, grieving a "suicide" the reader knows was murder, quietly closes a loop Part 1 opened in its second chapter: Emil killed Travis Teh in that office, Chanta cleaned it up, Sarah moved in. Now we see the other side — the family, the grief, the official lie. The disc's chain of custody gains another link without a single line of exposition.

**Alexander's interrogation of Tony (Ch14) is the Part's best character scene.** In Part 1, Alexander saved Chanta's life at the bomb site — kicked a sniper rifle twice, accidentally killed a captain. Now he's arresting the same man. Two drinking associates performing a formal interrogation neither believes in. Alexander's entrapment tactics — the hospital-name trap, reframing Chanta's silences as confessions — are clever enough to work on someone less stubborn. And Chanta's refusal to break is the scene that earns his arc: the man who couldn't string a sentence together around Sarah in Part 1 now chooses decades of imprisonment over betraying her location. That's not urchin cunning; that's love. The class dynamics are sharp too — Alexander uses physical intimacy (hand on thigh) as a dominance gesture, and Chanta can't name what's happening to him because his vocabulary doesn't have words for structural power.

**Halo's two scenes (Ch15) are the novel's darkest writing — and where Halo finally works.** His Part 1 introduction over-signalled him: "psycho-killer monotone" told the reader too much too early. Part 2 is where the character clicks, merging the menace with absurdity. The chapter opens with Halo mid-kill — the Point of Order doing its work — and the prose stays clinical. The intervals between strikes are getting shorter, the pleasure duller. Only Sarah would count. Then scene two reframes murder as holiday: the Commissioner's assassination is described retrospectively — body to dog food factory, faeces-stuffed effigies burned on church lawns — with the domestic contentment of someone recounting a spa weekend. The Avi Grubler flashback ("I come so hard when I blow things up") retroactively connects Halo to the Stickett Inn bombing. And the knitting digression — needles remind him of stabbing, yarn of garrotting, "even the fluffiest mittens had proved a gateway drug back to recreational murder" — is comedy so black it creates its own event horizon. This is the Halo the novel needs: not a signposted predator, but a man whose hobbies include lawn bowls, political assassination, and, when things are slow, recreational murder.

**The pregnancy reveal (Ch19) is beautifully calibrated.** Remember that two-word plant buried in Sarah's Part 1 med-exam results? PREGNANCY: negative. Eighteen chapters later, Kathy arrives in pigtails and scrubs, and the impossibility of the situation lands with full force. Sarah's first reaction isn't joy or grief — it's forensic analysis, then class anxiety ("a Cloudborne's child"). Kathy pushes back on the classism. Sarah confesses everything — the bombing, PAPER, the Memphis Mafia Bar, the Vegas Show, Tony's torture. It's the first time Sarah has told the whole story to anyone, and the scene lets the confession run uncompressed, at the length it asks for. After twelve chapters of Part 1 where Sarah navigated everything alone ("short on available friends"), Kathy's arrival gives her a genuine confidante for the first time in the novel.

**Eloise's arrival (Ch21, Scene 1) is a power scene that reshapes the novel.** Her entrance — "Picture your grandmother. Now give her glossy white hair spun with gold" — is the narrator at full stretch. Then Eloise demands the Gyges Disc, and when Sarah hesitates, snaps her fingers and kills one of her own contractors (Alexa) to demonstrate consequences. She takes the disc, hands it to Johnny Stoat (who works for her — a betrayal Sarah feels immediately), and then delivers Tony as a "gift." In a single scene, Eloise establishes herself as the novel's most dangerous character: she's smarter than Jones, more ruthless than Halo, and more powerful than both combined. She arrives fully operational from her first scene — no easing-in, no ramp.

**The Elvi breakout (Ch20) is the Part's best action setpiece.** Tulsa McLean's tank smashing through the wall of Truth Central at 9:05am — one minute into the morning's scheduled torture — is perfectly timed. The novel earns this by first establishing Tony's daily routine (wake-up 6am, cold shower 6:30, breakfast gruel 7, make-up 7:30, green room 8:30, interrogation and torture 9am sharp), so the reader feels the precision of the rescue's timing. Halo's parallel plan (he was going to do it himself, had the skeleton keys) creates retroactive tension and "supernova-grade fury." The camera operator who keeps filming while everyone else flees is a lovely plant. And Jones fleeing the building — his second humiliation by the Elvi — tracks his arc downward with elegant precision.

**The DNA reveal (Ch22) is the novel's best twist so far.** Clinton Richard Dawkins. Not Tony's child. Not even Sarah's, genetically — the foetus is a perfect clone with zero copying errors and viral editing. Every reader expectation is overturned simultaneously. And the reveal is delivered through Kathy's lab work, which means it arrives as science, not prophecy. The prophecy demanded a child named Clinton; the DNA says the child IS Clinton, genetically. That's a different kind of fulfilment, and it reframes the Sages from mystics into something more unsettling. The "Origins of Holy Momma" narrator essay that follows — tying the reveal to April 25th (UNSAC Day and Gladys Love's birthday) — is a bravura digression carried by sheer voice.

**"Evolve Me Tender" (Ch22, Scene 4) is the Part's secret weapon.** Tony, about to be evicted by Elvi who've learned he's not the father, spontaneously writes a song. It comes from nowhere — "words seemingly coming from nowhere" — and it stops the Elvi cold. Then the Elvi start arguing over their own lyrics ("Load my blender," "Lathe my fender," "Love lavender"), and the scene pivots from threat to musical comedy. This is the origin of Chanta as the Elvi's "Teacher," the role that pays off in Part 3's "Let Your Momma Go." The scene does three things at once: it resolves the eviction crisis, establishes Tony's creative gift, and gives the Elvi their first moment of genuine cultural identity beyond military posturing. And it does it through a song about evolution and genetics — thematically perfect for a chapter that just revealed the baby is a clone.

## What Part 2 Holds Back

What looks like a gap in Part 2 is almost always deliberate withholding — the staged revelation the book runs on. The Sages never appear in person across the whole Part; their prophecy drives the pregnancy, the DNA reveal, and the Elvi's mobilisation while they stay offstage, and the reader carries their menace forward as a memory from Part 1's drugging scene. Coming after a Part-1 confrontation and ahead of an early Part-3 return (Ch25), that's the mythological engine resting between detonations, not a hole in the board. The same schedule governs the players introduced in a minor key to deepen later — Hayley reacting to Alexander's absurdity, Chip's stasis-anomaly thread, Gerry's quiet rise to Commissioner and the reveal that he's a deep PAPER plant. None is fully drawn here because none is meant to be yet: "you will not be given things straight" is a release schedule as much as a tone.

**The reconciliation scene (Ch21, Scene 2) shifts the bond into a new register.** Across Part 1 the Sarah-Chanta connection lived in tactile specificity (she bathes his burns, buys him socks, exfoliates his head with a giraffe tongue) — the closeness of two people who will not cross the gulf between them. The reunion works in a different key. Sarah hits him, accuses him of rape, he swears he was unconscious — the accusation gives the scene genuine tension and reshapes the bond around the pregnancy rather than the old domestic intimacy. The freight is heavy — disc, Sages, pregnancy — and the feeling is carried by the accusation and the dialogue rather than by the physical detail that defines them elsewhere (fairy boxers, vodka aftershave, the giraffe tongue, "Mr Shiny-Brilliant-Moonwater"). Beside Part 3's finale, where Chanta's grief is earned through duration, silence, and physical specificity, the register here is verbal and forensic — fitting for a relationship redrawn by distance, if less viscerally felt than the tactile mode.

## Voice

Part 2's voice is the novel's most varied. Six distinct registers operate across its twenty scenes:

**Sarah's domestic siege** — morning sickness as combat, the mansion as gilded cage, the Elvi as devoted jailers. The Dr Nick's Mix sequence (dancing naked, glass in hair, forehead on shower drain) is physical comedy that also communicates desperation.

**Alexander's theatrical delusion** — every scene he inhabits becomes a production. His interrogation of Tony, his visit to Hayley, his phone call about the SSS — all filtered through showrunner logic. "Is a police officer really a police officer if they don't have a show?" is the novel's most efficient character line.

**Halo's clinical menace** — colder than the rest of the novel, deliberately unfunny in its violence but blackly comic in its framing (vacation, lawn bowls, knitting as gateway drug). The novel wisely never lets Halo wink.

**Gerry's dry interiority** — his Ch18 introduction is filtered entirely through his own wry, anxious POV: the necrotic-mirror view from his sub-Layer office, Dunhilton's face as an insult to most salamanders, the gallows-humour idiom of "pillowing" a man out a window, and the "Gerry at home, Very among agency colleagues" gag that seeds the running "Very Hard Man" pun (and Jones's "G. Hardman" → "G hard man" → "Jihad Man" doodle — a genuinely clever bit of code-breaking). The controlled, deadpan register — performed deference over a clocking interior — is voice by design, the seed of the three-register operator he becomes in later Parts.

**Ashby-Prynne's corporate predation** — appearing in both Ch17 and Ch22, escalating from "Wet Blouse Wednesday" to Thornberry stealing Sarah's work to "Project Positivity Enhancement" involving photographers at low filing drawers. He's a two-scene satirical portrait that builds, not a one-off.

**The narrator's essayistic mode** — the "Origins of Holy Momma" passage (UNSAC Day, Gladys Love's birthday, the Journal of Retrospective Busy-Bodying) is the novel's most sustained narrator intrusion in Part 2, and it works because the voice is so confident. The narrator is having fun, and the reader can tell.

## The Threads

Part 2 is managing eight narrative threads and handles the load through discipline about which threads get focus. The primary threads — Sarah's pregnancy, Tony's imprisonment, Halo's escalation — get the most ink. The secondary threads — Chip's introduction, Gerry's rise, the SSS investigation — are planted and left to grow.

The triangle-of-pursuers structure — established in Part 1 with Jones (wants the disc), Halo (wants to kill Sarah), and Alexander (wants to romance her) — is now fully operational. None of the three knows about the others. Alexander arrests Tony while Jones pursues him for a different reason, while Halo plots to rescue him for a third. The Elvi cut through all three by acting first. Then Eloise cuts through the Elvi by arriving with more power than anyone expected. Part 1 planted this triangle; Part 2 activates it, then introduces a fourth force (Eloise) that outranks all three. It's elegantly layered — four forces, each thinking it's in control, each wrong.

The Gyges Disc thread transitions cleanly: Sarah hands it to Eloise (who hands it to Johnny), setting up Part 3's testing sequence. Tony's arc makes its most important transition: from passive victim (imprisoned, rescued) to active creator (writing songs, earning the Elvi's devotion). "Evolve Me Tender" is the hinge — after that scene, the Elvi see Chanta differently, and so does the reader.

## Verdict

Part 2 is the novel's detonation sequence — ten chapters that systematically invert every assumption Part 1 spent twelve chapters establishing. Where Part 1 taught the reader how to read the novel (violence and comedy coexist, digressions are features, the narrator is your unreliable friend), Part 2 teaches the reader not to trust what they've learned. Sarah can't get pregnant — she's pregnant. Tony is a passive cleaner — he's a songwriter who can stop a roomful of Elvi mid-brawl. The Sages are harmless eccentrics — they cloned Richard Dawkins into Sarah's uterus. Alexander saved Chanta's life — now he's arresting him. Part 2's structural achievement is making every Part 1 certainty feel provisional.

Its strengths are concentrated in its character scenes (Alexander-Tony interrogation, pregnancy reveal, Halo's "vacation," Eloise's disc seizure, "Evolve Me Tender") and the Chip-Teh connection that closes Part 1's opening loop. Its apparent gaps are deliberate withholding rather than fault — the Sages rested offstage, the detectives and operators planted in a minor key to deepen later. Its one most load-bearing choice is the reconciliation, which carries its heavy freight — disc, Sages, pregnancy — through a verbal register where Part 1 worked through touch.

Part 3 inherits a loaded board: Sarah pregnant and isolated, Tony rescued and now the Elvi's songwriter, Halo furious and escalating, Hayley planted as an investigator not yet unleashed, Jones twice-humiliated, Eloise in possession of the disc and demonstrated as the novel's most powerful player, and a prophesied child whose genetic father has been dead for centuries. Part 1 built the world; Part 2 broke it open.
