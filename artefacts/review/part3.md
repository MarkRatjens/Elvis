# Part 3 — Developmental Review

Date: 2026-03-06 | Chapters 23–39 (17 chapters) | Developmentally edited: Ch34–39

---

## The Shape

Seventeen chapters. Part 1 built the world in twelve chapters. Part 2 detonated it in ten. Part 3 is what happens in the rubble — and it's the longest Part so far because rubble takes time to sort.

The Part opens with political manoeuvring (Gerry, Hayley) and domestic pressure (Alexander courting Sarah, Sarah considering abortion), weaves through a sprawling investigation, an arms-dealer arrival, a military assault, and closes on a man sitting in ruins singing a blues song about letting go. That's a hell of an arc. Where Part 2 moved fast because it was inverting Part 1's certainties, Part 3 moves methodically because it's establishing new ones: who has the disc and what it does, who the real killer is, who controls the weapons, and what happens to two people who can't cross the gulf between them when Eloise Shakti drives a limousine straight through the middle of it.

By the final page, every major character has lost or abandoned something they were holding.

## What Part 3 Does Right

**Hayley's investigation is the Part's backbone — and the payoff for Part 2's patience.** In Part 2, Hayley was planted but not dramatised — a voice of reason reacting to Alexander's absurdity in two office scenes. Part 3 unleashes her. She carries at least eight of the seventeen chapters, and they work because her methodology is specific and procedural without being dull. She visits real locations. She examines real evidence. She catches real tells (the concierge's demeanour ossifying the moment she mentions police). The fairy boxers she finds at both Tony's and Sarah's apartments — a pristine Floormart six-pack at Sarah's, worn ones at Tony's — are the kind of physical detail this novel runs on, the same tonal register as Part 1's best comedy (class difference expressed through underwear). Her near-assassination at Halo's apartment is the Part's best action setpiece — not because of the violence, but because of how the violence validates her detective work. PAPER doesn't strafe apartments because you're wrong.

**Johnny's disc testing** is the Part's best science-fiction sequence. The progression from radiation tests to the frisbee accident to the spinning apparatus to the eruption is mechanically precise and narratively escalating. The kinetic discovery feels earned because it's accidental — the science doesn't cooperate until someone drops something. The eruption is genuinely frightening. And the fractal pattern on the observation window is a setup planted with enough mystery to sustain the reader through everything that follows.

**Eloise's Part 3 escalation completes her Part 2 introduction.** She arrived in Part 2 and killed a contractor to take the disc. In Part 3, she goes further: she proposes that Sarah marry Alexander as a succession play, then demonstrates she can shut down every weapon on the battlefield — PAPER's gunships and the Elvi's tanks simultaneously. The John Deere analogy, the customer-service-tone threats to Jones, the silent assessment of the women in the back seat — this is a character who reshuffles the deck just by sitting down. The firmware reveal is the novel's best twist since the Dawkins DNA because it works the same way: it reframes everything retrospectively. Every weapon the Elvi used in Part 2's breakout, every tank PAPER deployed, was always on Eloise's leash. The reader re-evaluates two Parts of action in a single scene.

**Gerry is one of the Part's quiet pleasures.** His scenes are comic-operator writing of a high order. "Gerry proposes a solution to the Elvi problem" (Ch23) is a highlight: he manoeuvres Dunhilton into handing him total command of PAPER and TRAMPS while managing every scrap of what the CEO is allowed to know, and closes on the purest line of his register — "Gerry finally sipped his whisky and said nothing. In matters like these, silence was the most diplomatic lie." The wallaby-piss Tasmanian "Scotch" forced on him at midday, the "Parramatta" → "your Serene Salamander Face" murmured at a dead screen, the Warren Zevon bait his boss can't catch ("And their hair was perfect"), the travelling-salesman smile working Siddig out of the bomb-site lease, the "My idea. That's my idea" theft of a junior's solution — this is character comedy that happens to carry thriller plot, the novel's best mode. The Gerry–Dunhilton scenes are comic power-theatre: Gerry humours a blustering boss he fully expects to outlast, in a controlled deadpan register that is unmistakably his.

**The cheeseburger.** The Part's ending is perfect. After 17 chapters of escalating stakes, conspiracies, military attacks, and heartbreak, the final line is a man asking for fast food. It's the most Australian thing in the novel, and it does more tonal work than any amount of atmospheric prose could.

## How Part 3 Is Built

The middle (Ch26–32) cross-cuts four threads in parallel — Hayley's procedural (the Stickett Inn, the apartments), Sarah's interiority (the abortion she considers, the nightmares), Gerry's lab operations (opening the HIBReLabs, acquiring the bomb site), and the disc testing — and it detonates at its centre: the kinetic eruption (Ch31), the Part's best science-fiction sequence, re-rules the disc, vaporises a lab, and leaves the fractal pattern etched into the observation window. The Part dwells here because the work is accretive — establishing who has the disc and what it does, who the killer is, who controls the weapons — and four distinct voices carry the dwell at once (Clavell/Vidal in scale, Pratchett/Adams in method).

Two threads run in reserve. The Sages get one standout scene (Ch25 — the foetal-implanting confrontation, the apocalyptic "One After the One," the parodic gifts of tarnished two-dollar coin and half-used analgesic tube) and then withdraw until Part 4. Halo, after Part 2's vacation chapter, stays offstage — investigated through Hayley's raid on his stripped, display-suite apartment, never encountered — held for his Part 4 return. The book reveals on its own schedule: "you will not be given things straight" is a schedule as much as a tone.

**Alexander's siege is character delivered through television.** His arc across three Parts is a controlled descent — accidental heroism (kicking Sman's rifle in Part 1), active antagonism (interrogating Chanta in Part 2), showrunner delusion fully unmoored from reality here. The siege plays as a Channel Forty-Eight "exclusive" broadcast: police ring the Ghetto mansion, and Alexander hijacks the coverage as a "Candid Criminal special," mangling the journalist Blake Mudrake into "Drake," trashing PAPER's "incompetence" live to camera — "Look at my face — this is a face you can trust," the narrator clocking "a sleepy koala manufacturing a smile for a goanna" — and breaking character only to snarl at his producer, "Has this idiot even read the script?" The menace is genuine — a dozen PAPER gunships hover unnoticed in the Layer above him throughout — but it reaches the reader as coverage, a broadcast carnival refracted through Alexander's certainty that a siege is content. It is the showrunner delusion in its native medium, the literal extension of "is a police officer really a police officer if they don't have a show." The open work is execution: whether the broadcast-circus register lands as character satire on the page.

**Sarah's descent is the spine of her Part 3.** In Part 1 she was the novel's engine — investigating Jones on her holoscreen, tracking Chanta through the Pale, navigating the Ghetto, confronting the Sages, carrying the disc through a bar fight — and the narrative kept dropping a word it never explained: "invincible." Here a pregnancy that by her own account shouldn't be possible brings her low for the first time, and she copes badly, with no practice at being weak. The beats trace the fall: she considers an abortion, confronts the Sages over the foetal implanting ("Which one of you taxers raped me?" is vintage Sarah), is fired, suffers the fractal-pattern nightmares, is proposed to, is carried from the mansion by Eloise — while the old engine of her flickers through, in the Sages confrontation (Ch25) and the window she vaults athletically in Ch38, "sick of being handled like an invalid." Part 3's task here is to draw the debt — the gap between "invincible" and felled, alongside Chanta's unexplained healing and the broken "can't get pregnant" — and to leave it unpaid.

The open work is a craft one, reader-safe because it turns on the page and not the withheld why: keeping the descent voiced from inside. "Sarah, who could once have put a fist through the wall, lets herself be carried because the fight's gone and she hates that in herself" holds the fall within her; the same beats narrated from outside would slacken into mere handling. Where the prose already works from inside, it wants nothing further.

## The Threads

Part 3 is juggling eleven narrative threads (by the arc tracker's count), and the load is controlled. The threads grounded in specific character action carry most clearly: Hayley investigating, Eloise manoeuvring, Chanta grieving. Others run in reserve by design — the Sages quiet after Ch25, Halo offstage after Part 2, both held for Part 4. Gerry's political machinery is some of the Part's sharpest comic-operator writing (above).

Halo runs in reserve. Part 2 delivered the novel's darkest writing with him — the vacation chapter, the knitting digression, the lawn bowls, menace fused with absurdity. Part 3 keeps him offstage: investigated but never encountered, with Hayley's raid on his stripped, display-suite apartment the Part's best action sequence, his return held for Part 4. With him gone, Part 3's visceral threat comes from institutions — PAPER, Shakti — a colder register of danger by design.

## Voice

The voice across Part 3 is consistently the author's, and it's evolved. Part 1 established the voice through bravura digressions (experience sheaths, hover-copts, Floormart). Part 2 compressed the digressions and let character voice carry more weight (Alexander's showrunner delusion, Halo's vacation register, Ashby-Prynne's corporate predation). Part 3 goes further still: the digressions are gone, and voice operates almost entirely through character action and dialogue. The Australian register is strong (beetroot, cheeseburger, "go a"), the narrator-intrusion gags land (Alexander's rank-garbling, the Dr John/Dr Nick injection standoff), but the narrator is less present than in Parts 1 or 2. The prose has matured from showy to confident.

The novel's best voice-mode — deadpan absurdism in the middle of genuine stakes — reaches its peak in Part 3 with Eloise's phone call to Agent Jones. That scene is the novel firing on all cylinders: tension, comedy, character, world-building, and a John Deere tractor reference, simultaneously. And "Let Your Momma Go" achieves something new for the novel — sincerity without ironic protection. Part 1's Vegas Show approached this (Sarah's state of grace), but the narrator still framed it. Part 3's finale lets Chanta's grief and song stand without commentary. The cheeseburger is the only deflection, and it's earned.

## Verdict

Part 3 is the novel's pivot — the point where it stops being a comic thriller about a cleaner and a pregnant woman hiding from spies, and becomes an epic about arms dealers, firmware backdoors, and the political economy of cloned Elvis impersonators. Part 1 built the world; Part 2 broke it open; Part 3 reveals the power structures underneath. It handles the transition with more grace than it has any right to.

Part 3's character is in its cross-cutting: a middle that runs four voices at once and detonates the disc at its centre, Gerry's comic operations, the Sages and Halo held in reserve for Part 4, and Sarah's descent dramatised beat by beat. The open work is execution — whether Sarah's diminishment and Alexander's broadcast-siege land on the page in the registers they're built for.

Its strengths — Hayley's investigation, Eloise's escalation, the Sages' confrontation, Chanta's grief, the disc eruption — are among the novel's best work. And "Let Your Momma Go" achieves something the novel has been building toward since "Evolve Me Tender": Chanta's transformation from a man who couldn't string a sentence together around Sarah into a songwriter who can transform a community's grief into release. That arc, measured across three Parts, is the novel's most complete character achievement so far.

Part 4 inherits a clean setup: Sarah in Eloise's orbit, Chanta in the ruins with pacified Elvi, Hayley nearly killed and now dangerous, Jones humiliated, Halo dormant but overdue, and a fractal pattern nobody understands yet. Sarah and Chanta are at maximum physical separation but, after "Let Your Momma Go," at a new emotional equilibrium across the gulf they will not cross. That's a strong hand to play from.
