**Option A — Adams-forward (absurd deflation)**
> In a Ghetto full of Elvi, Rex Humble stood out like the one goldfish in the bowl with a moustache. He was a fan — a human, who had loved the Elvi for years without a single one of the qualifications, and who had spent his Tuesdays at open-mic nights doing the sort of thing to a microphone that normally required a licence. He'd done his hair and grooming after the clean-cut Elvi of the early series releases: the plain suit, the hair coaxed upward with more faith than product. The effect was a man who sold you certainty on a channel you didn't remember subscribing to — pastel, coiffed, lightly basted for broadcast. Behind a pulpit that three weeks ago had been the lectern where a maître d' turned away men in the wrong shoes, he smiled the smile you bought with money you hadn't earned and had blessed on the way in the door.

**Option B — Vonnegut-forward (flat cosmic shrug; your edited opening)**
> There are men who are called to preach, there are men to whom preaching is merely the first thing they ever turned out to be good at, and there are men who are neither of those, but recognise a good chance to build a TV empire out of it anyway. Rex Humble was firmly the third. He was a nobody, in the sense the Ghetto cared about: a human, a fan, one of the many who loved the Elvi without the equipment for it, and who had sung at open-mic nights with a sincerity that cleared rooms. He resembled the kind of man who appears on television very late at night to explain what is wrong with you and how much the cure will cost. When the schism came and the Ghetto filled with the newly devout, Rex Humble recognised an opening in the market and filled it. It is an easy mistake, from the outside, to take a man who has found his customers for a man who has found God. The symptoms are identical.

**Option C — Pratchett-forward (literalised machinery, Chanta-anchored)**
> The thing about Pastor Rex Humble — the thing Chanta understood in one queasy lurch — was that he was a fan. Everyone else in the room was an Elvi, give or take a sideburn; Humble was the human who had come to watch and stayed to run the place. He had loved the Elvi loudly, publicly and without noticeable talent for most of his adult life, and had been rewarded, as such men occasionally are, for standing in the right doorway when the schism came looking for a leader. He'd modelled himself on the clean-cut Elvi of the early series releases and come out looking like a man beamed in to explain a payment plan: the pink suit, the hair lacquered in the early-series manner, the teeth arranged for a camera that wasn't in the room yet but soon would be. And he was shearing them. One by one the Elvi knelt, and one by one Humble relieved them of the sideburns they'd been decanted with — because nothing settles a shepherd like a flock that has agreed, in writing, to hold still.

## The shears — the joke (goes at the ritual beat, not the portrait)

You floated scissors → electric shaver → "biblically-appropriate shears," and set the brief: he **calls** them the "Biblical Shears," **prefers** them to electric clippers (there must be a joke for the preference), and they are really just **stationery scissors from Officejerks**. Two builds of the gag — the preference-joke is the load-bearing part:

**Build 1 — "plugged into Babylon" (doctrine vs. the power bill):**
> He called them the Biblical Shears, and he preferred them, he preached, to the electric clipper — whose buzz he had identified as the drone of the Adversary, and whose need for a power point he took as proof it was plugged directly into Babylon. The Shears asked no such compromise with the world. They were a pair of orange-handled scissors he had bought at Officejerks, from a bin marked THREE FOR $5, and had not, in the strictest accounting, needed three.

**Build 2 — "Number Three guard comb" (Samson, deflated):**
> "Nowhere in Scripture," he was fond of thundering, "does the good Lord reach for a Number Three guard comb." This was true. ## Guardrails for any drafting pass
- **Respectful-lampoon rule (non-negotiable):** target the apparatus (grift, modesty-policing, weaponised faith, collection plate), NEVER the gospel or the converts' genuine rapture. The hymns are the real thing — the homecoming the [[../world-rules/social-structure|Grayling warranty]] denied the Elvi. See [[../research/elvis-gospel-milieu|Gospel Milieu]].
- **He means it.** Humble is a sincere believer, not a wink-to-camera con man. The comedy is the sincerity. Do not play him as a knowing fraud.
- **A human fan; never singing.** Not a clone — he apes the Elvi (dresses and grooms toward the clean-cut early series) but reads televangelist. He sang (badly) in the past only; **do not have him sing in the present, least of all Chanta's songs.**
- **All real referents stay off page.** Never name Rex Humbard, Rex Dyson, the baptism, or any real preacher in the prose; deploy them only as look, apparatus, and structural rhyme (see [[../characters/humble|character doc]] Model note). "Impersonator" is a notes word, not a page word — show the open-mic past, don't label it.
- House basics: Australian spelling; "tax" as the Elvi expletive; Chanta-POV naming = "Chanta"; em-dashes only where the rhythm earns them.
