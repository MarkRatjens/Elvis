# Part 1 — Developmental Review

Date: 2026-03-06 | Chapters 1–12 (12 chapters, 26 scenes) | Surveyed, not developmentally edited

---

## The Shape

Twelve chapters, twenty-six scenes. Part 1 is the novel's longest sustained act of world-building, and it knows it. The opening gambit is audacious: two parallel introductions that don't connect for several chapters. Sarah celebrates her promotion at an Elvi karaoke bar (Ch1), then the narrative cuts to Emil murdering Travis Teh in an office tower and dying of a heart attack in the street (Ch2). The connection is a coin-sized disc of what looks like old boot leather. By the time Chapter 3 brings Sarah and Chanta together over the same crime scene — she's the new occupant of the dead man's office, he's the cleaner scrubbing the shotgun suicide — the novel has established its method: comedy and violence share the same room, and neither apologises for the other.

From there, Part 1 spirals outward. Jones pursues the disc. Halo pursues Sarah. Alexander pursues celebrity. The Sages pursue prophecy. Sarah pursues answers. Chanta pursues Sarah. And in the final three chapters, the part detonates everything it's been setting up: the Memphis Mafia Bar raid, the Vegas Show, the Sages' drugging, the disc attacking Sarah, and Chanta's miraculous healing and kidnapping. Part 1 closes on two cliffhangers and a man wandering out of the Ghetto to buy coffee.

The title — "Your Aunt Buffing up a Groundhog" — is the part's first mondegreen, and it announces the book's relationship with the reader: you will not be given things straight.

## What Part 1 Does Right

**The opening paragraph is a mission statement.** "You'd think Sarah, being Airborne, would celebrate in appropriate Airborne fashion" — then the narrator spends a paragraph demolishing that expectation. By the time we reach "Sarah, short on available friends this night, invited Johnny, best described as an awkward acquaintance," we know three things: the narrator is unreliable in the best way, the world is stratified, and the protagonist doesn't belong where she should. "She was, after all, invincible" drops two sentences later, planted so casually the reader can't know it's literal.

**Emil's chapter (Ch2) is a perfect short story embedded in the part.** A dying man murders someone, steals a disc, goes to the pub, and drops dead of a heart attack. "Instead of being executed, Emil Kalheri decided he'd rather go to the pub" — that's the part's voice in one sentence. The scene does everything: introduces the disc, establishes that PAPER kills casually, sets up the chain of custody (Teh → Emil → street → Chanta), and delivers a death that's simultaneously pathetic and dignified. Emil seeing the moon for the first time — "He'd never seen it before, and it seemed unfairly famous" — is the part's first moment of genuine beauty, arriving in the middle of a murder scene. That's the book's signature move.

**Sarah and Chanta's meeting (Ch3) is a masterclass in romantic comedy.** The fairy boxers. The breezy brains. "Got a cyborg sniffer in your handbag?" "Do cyborgs smell more or less like chicken?" The scene builds chemistry through class friction — Sarah is authoritative, Chanta is mortified, and the power dynamic inverts twice (she's curious about him, he's ashamed; he knows things about her new office she doesn't, she's defensive). The disc's chain of custody extends when Chanta pockets it off Emil's corpse, and the reader doesn't yet know what it is. Meanwhile, Rick and Dick watch from their fake ambulance, cackling over meat pies, and the part establishes that surveillance in this world is performed by the most incompetent people imaginable. The Enchanted Cleaning Services name — "I'm magic at clean-ups" — is a character note disguised as a business card.

**Constable Sman's demographic culling (Ch6) is the part's most savage satire.** The concept — a police sniper so committed to equitable killing that he maintains live pivot tables during mass shootings — is horrifying and hilarious in equal measure. "Even the demos don't care about white hip hop artists" is a line that earns its cruelty by committing fully to the logic of its premise. The scene also does critical plot work: Alexander sees Sarah unconscious at the bomb site and falls in love, kicks Sman's rifle twice to save Chanta (accidentally killing Captain Junkers in the process), and the part establishes that Alexander's capacity for genuine human connection exists in direct proportion to his capacity for absurd incompetence. The Junkers death — the bullet taking "the scenic route through the bowel" — is the part's goriest comedy, and it works because the narrator treats it as an administrative inconvenience.

**The Experience Sheath digression (Ch8) is the part's best world-building set piece.** The Playful Therapeutics Consortium, the "insufficient novelty in full-body masturbation" origin story, the Console's pre-loaded narrative overlays ("Burning Cock," "I Never Knew Weevils Could Get in There," "Feel of Dropped Pie, Just as You Were About to Eat It") — this is Pratchettian world-building at its most confident. The digression runs long, but it earns its length because the comedy carries the horror: this is a government torture system, and the part describes it like a consumer electronics review. The shift when the clinician starts volunteering to administer Burning Cock — "Acquiring a taste for Agent Jones' brutality" — is one of the part's sharpest character observations, buried inside what looks like a comic aside.

**The Vegas Show (Ch12) is the part's first transcendent sequence.** Sarah enters the amphitheatre and the prose lifts: "music that refused transcendence, music that made you love the world's fingerprints and bruises." For a part that runs on deadpan comedy and satirical violence, this is the first moment where the prose allows itself to be unironically beautiful. Sarah feeling "she was one filament in a never-ending chain, love passed through music, rhinestone, wrestling, song" — that's the part's emotional thesis, stated without protective irony. And the surrounding architecture supports it: the Blue Hawaii allergic reaction on the way in (comedy), the Oath of the Warranty (comedy and world-building), the lyric fights on stage (comedy), and then the soloist whose voice reduces Sarah to tears (beauty). The tonal range in a single scene is extraordinary.

**The Sages are the part's best comic ensemble.** Kleo's beneficent smiles, Solo's earnest diplomacy, Chilo's compulsive blurting — they function as a unit that's simultaneously ridiculous and threatening. The Vegas Show scene where they drug Sarah and Chanta's drinks is a turning point precisely because the reader has been trained to laugh at them. "Be a good chap and distract the marks" is a line that does triple duty: it's funny, it reveals Chilo as the operator, and it plants a major plot bomb (the drugging leads to the pregnancy). The Sages operate in the part's most productive register: comedy that conceals genuine menace.

**Agent Jones's interior monologue (Ch8, "I am not human") is the part's bravest writing.** "Ever since he was a kid, he ached to please women. Which is why he hated them so much." In a part that runs on comedy, giving the primary antagonist a raw, ugly origin scene — misogynistic, violent, self-justifying — is a tonal risk. It works because the part doesn't ask the reader to sympathise, only to understand the mechanism. Jones isn't humanised; he's anatomised. The passage reads like a clinical report from inside a predator's skull, and the part never loses control of its disgust.

## The Sarah–Chanta Gulf

The central relationship is a study in an unbridgeable divide, and the divide is the subject. Sarah is a tourist at the edge of a life she will visit and decline; the text marks her drift plainly, her time among the Cloudborne becoming "less adventure and more voyeuristic tourism with each passing day." The caretaking that fills the recovery chapters — bathing Chanta's burns, the giraffe-tongue exfoliation, buying him socks and drinkable vodka, the Floormart pasta-mountain body — is tenderness conducted across a border she keeps. It is real, and it arrives at its own limit.

That limit is fixed by a single scene. In "Anthony Chantara," the two orbit each other, "the universe paused," the moon-water name softens her, and the moment opens: "if I had any sense, I'd let you talk me into it. But apparently, I haven't got that tonight." Chanta wants to close the space and stays "respectful of the alarm behind her eyes." Sarah leaves. The next scene opens the door on the consequence — "All the unsaid things remained unsaid, and a new detente made the spaces between them safe" — and the gulf is set in place for good. This is the scene the whole relationship turns on: one approach, declined with care, and a divide the part keeps faith with from here.

## The Disc

The disc's early behaviour traces a consistent kinetic rule, revealed by escalation rather than stated. In Ch2, Emil tosses it and the bar shakes. In Ch3, Chanta pockets it and it "sprang slovenly back into shape," inert under gentle handling. In Ch9, Sarah tosses it spinning and the van flips. In Ch11, it "glowed hot" in her bag when she touches it. In Ch12, it attacks Sarah, fusing to her nipple. The throughline is that force and motion wake it while idle handling leaves it dormant — a rule the reader can assemble on a second pass and that Part 3's testing sequence later makes explicit. The gaps between activations are the mystery doing its work: the disc is kept opaque on purpose while its chain of custody carries the plot.

## Voice

Part 1's voice is the part's most confident achievement. Five registers operate simultaneously:

**The narrator's comic omniscience.** "Eloise is for later in the story" — the narrator breaks the fourth wall without breaking the reader's immersion. "In less mutant universes, the tune would be recognised as The Wonder of You" — the multiverse conceit is deployed as a running gag, not a plot device, and the reader accepts it because the narrator's tone never wavers. The conceit is never explained, and the restraint is the point: the narrators are multiversal historians with their own unmentioned axes, a fact the reader is never handed, which keeps the intrusions principled rather than loose. The Floormart description, the hover-copt description, the experience sheath essay — these digressions work because the narrator treats absurd world-building with the deadpan authority of a technical manual.

**Sarah's assertive competence.** "Men ain't nothin' but dicks and hormones." "A colossal homicidal liar." "Tax me into poverty." Sarah's voice operates through rapid assessment — she reads people, names them, and moves on. Her conversations with Chanta are comic duets where she leads and he stumbles, but the part is careful to give her moments of vulnerability (avoiding bringing Chanta to her Airborne home, the fear behind her eyes when she leaves his apartment).

**Chanta's urchin cunning.** "It's an art form" (re: squalor). "You know you'll have to pay for that beer." Chanta's voice is reactive, short-burst, and unexpectedly precise when it matters. His cunning is social rather than intellectual — he reads situations faster than he can articulate them. The fairy boxers function as a voice marker: every time they appear, the reader knows the scene will run on embarrassment and charm.

**Jones's terse menace.** Present-tense declarations: "I am to sign some documents." "I will see the files now." "You will return what you stole." Jones speaks like a man who has never had a casual conversation. His voice gets its best moment in Ch8's interior monologue, where the terse exterior cracks open to reveal the ugly machinery underneath.

**Halo's clinical register.** Cold, precise, occasionally petulant. "Andrea deserves to die" — said flatly, as if commenting on the weather. His kill scenes are narrated with surgical specificity (the carotid triangle, the three-edged spike, the physics of arterial spray), and the clinical voice creates more dread than any amount of atmospheric description.

## The Threads

Part 1 plants eleven major narrative threads (by the ledger's count) and resolves six minor ones within the Part. The part's discipline is impressive: every thread planted in Part 1 pays off by Part 3, and most pay off within Part 2. The disc's chain of custody (Teh → Emil → street → Chanta → van → Sarah → bra → attacks Sarah) is the primary narrative engine, and it works because each transfer escalates the stakes while maintaining comic momentum.

The triangle of pursuers (Jones/Halo/Alexander) is established without any of the three knowing about the others. This is Part 1's strongest structural feature: three men converging on Sarah from three different motivations (Jones wants the disc, Halo wants to kill her, Alexander wants to romance her), creating a web of ironic tension that the reader can see but the characters can't.

The Sages' prophecy is planted late (Ch10) and escalated rapidly (Ch12), which gives Part 1 its back-weighted structure. Everything before Chapter 10 is world-building and character; everything after is mythology and escalation. This creates a Part that accelerates rather than decelerates, ending on its highest energy rather than winding down.

## Verdict

Part 1 is the novel's foundation, and it's built to last. Its strengths are concentrated in its voice (the narrator's comic omniscience, the deadpan absurdism, the Australian register), its character introductions (Sarah and Chanta's meeting, Alexander's bomb-site arrival, Jones's interior monologue, the Sages' proposition), and its climactic sequence (the Vegas Show through Chanta's kidnapping). The relationship at its centre is a gulf rather than a romance — Sarah and Chanta held apart by a divide the novel respects, fixed in place by a single approach declined with care.

The Part's deepest achievement is tonal: it teaches the reader how to read the novel. By the end of Part 1, the reader knows that violence and comedy coexist without apology, that digressions are features rather than flaws, that the narrator will break the fourth wall but never the story's emotional integrity, and that underneath the satirical dystopia there is no union to be had between a cleaner and a cyber-security expert who calls him Mr Fairy Boxers — only a gulf the novel honours. That's a lot of tonal calibration for twelve chapters, and Part 1 pulls it off.

Part 2 inherits a loaded hand: Sarah with the disc, Chanta kidnapped, Jones pursuing both, Halo hunting Sarah, Alexander smitten, the Sages having drugged their targets, and a PREGNANCY: negative that the reader has almost certainly forgotten. Part 1 dealt that hand slowly, but it dealt it right.

## Improvement Options

Most of Part 1 needs nothing. These are the changes that earn their place.

1. **Hold Halo's menace in reserve at his introduction.** His first appearance can keep the reader fooled the way his victims are, with the predatory machinery revealed once he begins hunting in earnest.
   - *Drinking-buddies scene (Ch5):* "Andrea is a fake Stacy bitch… She deserves to die" currently drops the table into silence, and that silence is the beat that turns mate-banter into dramatic irony. Let the table laugh it off — they already treat Halo as "a riot" — and he reads as a charming creep, the "psycho-killer monotone" playing as a bit rather than a tell.
   - *Avi scene (Ch5):* keep the pickup and the bomb interrupting them, but cut away at the mirror-glimpse, before the spike, and let Avi drawing the particle pistol first colour the moment as a hookup with a spook gone wrong. The serial-killer apparatus — the Point of Order, the carotid choreography, "Only Sarah would count" — moves into Halo's Part 2 thread, where the menace is meant to crystallise, so the spike was always in his hand on a reread.
   - *Preserve the plants by relocating, not deleting.* The murder is load-bearing where it sits — intercut with the bombing, and Avi turning out to be agency ("agency issue, professional," and "Avi would be missed") seeds that other hunters are in the field. Move the apparatus forward; keep the plants in place.


2. **Optional cross-Part plant — Sarah's physicality.** Part 4's combat reveal arrives cold, and Part 1 is its natural home for an advance payment. The seeds are already here — "She was, after all, invincible," and in the bar rescue she catches a shoved chair "easily, spun it back, and slid it under him." One low-stakes beat of unexpected speed or strength, salted in here, pre-pays the Part 4 fight at no cost and stays consistent with the invincibility already planted.
