# Part 4 — Developmental Review

Date: 2026-06-22 | Chapters 40–58 | Built from the canonical "Dearly Invested" scenes only

---

## The Shape

Part 4 is the Part where everyone commits, and where the novel spends most of its time getting the pieces into the positions Part 5 will play. It opens in domesticity — Eloise ferrying her new acquisitions out to Sublime Point, a devon rex named Chainsaw screaming in a carrier, Hayley losing a war with a forty-three-parameter espresso machine — and it closes in a service elevator with Sarah standing over a dead professional killer, his blood across her belly, while her new husband takes credit for the kill on camera. Between those two poles the book runs three tracks at once and lets them converge.

Sarah's track is institutional capture, and it is the one that genuinely moves. She arrives at Sublime Point a guest, is walked to a lookout and shown the Three Sisters, is locked in a holding cell over a fridge, and then turns the whole arrangement around at the negotiating table — not by escaping it but by seizing a position inside it, taking command of Eloise's disc project and naming her own terms. Chanta's track is the opposite: a holding pattern in a Ghetto recording studio, blocked, missing Sarah, waiting for inspiration that won't come, until the Elvi themselves manufacture a breakthrough out of Richard Dawkins. And the third track is the tightening of the threats around both of them — Jones building a paranoid conspiracy theory out of bar napkins, Gerry and Dunhilton setting a date for the genocide, Halo working his way toward Sarah in a waiter's jacket, Chip pulled out of Fridge Force and into the compound. All three converge at the Gravity Well, where the wedding reception becomes a bar brawl and the bar brawl becomes Halo's death.

## What Part 4 Does Right

**Sublime Point as a velvet cage.** The compound does the work the mansion did in earlier Parts, but trades the siege for hospitality, which unsettles in a quieter way. Nobody is locked in; they are simply invited to discover they have "inadvertently locked themselves outside," a gag the book runs three times and lands each time. The Arabica Armaments Model Seven espresso machine — forty-three parameters, an AI trained on Michelin chefs and "at least one PAPER interrogator," aggressive foam-stabilisation protocols — tells you everything about Eloise in a single appliance, the way the Experience Sheath did its owner earlier. And the slow defection of Hayley's cat to Eloise, won with a crystal dish of bluefin tuna and a "Who's a good Chainsaw?", is the compound's whole thesis rendered as comedy: everyone here is for sale, and Eloise already knows the price.

**The lookout (Sarah and Eloise).** The scene does a great deal of character work in a small space. Eloise walks Sarah out to a cliff platform, points at the Three Sisters across the valley — "if one ever cracked and fell, the others would follow" — and recruits her three women in one move while sounding like she's discussing geology. The corporate death-calculus is exact and horrible: employee mortality "down 3.4%," People and Culture pleased because "the dead don't break non-compete clauses." Underneath it runs Sarah's diminishment, dramatised in the body rather than announced — she moves "through slightly thicker air than everyone else," doesn't "trust her usual reflexes as much as she had a month ago," presses a hand to a belly where nothing has happened yet. And the scene refuses to tell you whether Eloise's storming exit was real: Sarah is left on the bench "not entirely sure the thunderstorm had been real," which is exactly how the book should handle Eloise — the theatre stays theatre, never confirmed as feeling. It closes on the descent image the whole arc is built around: whether the ground would "rise up to meet her the way it eventually met everything that flew too high for too long."

**The negotiation.** Sarah's lowest ebb is followed at once by a decisive counter. Cornered into the marriage, she calls Eloise's bluff by filing a Random Allocation application on her own — and Eloise has already rigged the system, so the form comes back allocating her to Alexander in record time. It's a small, clean demonstration of the rule the whole Part runs on: Eloise has thought of it first. But Sarah doesn't fold; she pivots the loss into leverage, extracts Kathy as her physician and Hayley as her security, and then takes the disc project for herself — "I want in. I want to run it." The diminished woman of the lookout is the same woman who walks out of this scene running a weapons-research division.

**The Chip recruitment.** Chip Stockwell is the new player Part 4 introduces, and the arc is patient with him. Two interviews at the Gravity Well escalate from professional courtesy to an explosive patch between his shoulder blades and the Chuglinghams produced alive at the next table as leverage; by the time he's integrated into the lab, the joke has become structural — a man whose whole craft is being underestimated, recruited by a woman whose craft is the same. His interrogation by Sarah ("twenty years monitoring anomalies... fifteen declined promotions... that's a lot of effort for someone who's comfortable") is two careful operators sizing each other up, and neither blinks. The capper is Eloise: she's already jailbroken his "encrypted" tablet, neutralised his private security, and traced his split signing bonus, and takes all of it as "confirmation that she'd hired the right man." His final beat — finding himself flagged a person of interest and opening his own stasis unit's panel, deciding it's "time to learn it from the inside" — turns the recruit into a player.

**Kathy on the Travelator.** A self-contained comic set piece that also does quiet plot work. Kathy mobilises an Elvi combat squad — martial artists in ponchos carrying guitars *and* semi-automatics — to wait on a platform for a train that arrives only when you stop wanting it to. The slouching-soldiers business is precise (one keeping "operational readiness" by resting a boot on his rifle stock), and Kathy's rant lands the absurdity cold: "I'm standing on a platform with an army of Elvis clones carrying semi-automatic rifles and guitars, waiting for a train that turns up when it feels like it, and I'm the one who looks out of place because I haven't got a poncho." And the Sages arrive with wheeled suitcases — they're relocating, not visiting — so the mythological furniture is physically moved into Eloise's compound while Kathy plays courier.

**The Dawkins religion.** The studio's other engine is the Elvi reading Richard Dawkins and building a religion out of an atheist. They take his attacks on faith as scripture, chant "Praise the One," declare his anti-prophecy a prophecy, and crown Chanta the teacher who "saw it immediately," all while he protests "No, it's not!" to no effect whatsoever. It's the novel's satire doing its central trick — institutions manufacturing the exact thing they claim to oppose — and it does it without a narrator stepping in to explain the joke.

**The inauguration (the non-reunion).** Sarah and Chanta share a dais for the first time in ten chapters, and the scene plays the gulf straight: the deadpan ("you look like a disco ball had a baby with a Medieval king"), the bodily comedy of late pregnancy, and the bare exchange that fixes the distance in place — "I miss you." / "I miss you too." / "It's impossible." The High Elvis's address that follows is a dense weave of Elvis-title mondegreens building self-determination rhetoric to a single deflating button: "We will clean up the Ghetto!" / "We're musicians, not street sweepers." The private beat and the public address are two movements of one event, and Sarah's pelvic-floor vigilance threaded through the speech keeps her present rather than sidelined.

**The wedding.** St Hedges gives the Latter Day Capitalism satire a long run — Accountancy Black Buffalo grass, a seating capacity estimated "between 3000 and 23 billion," a liturgy out of the Market Bible ("Exploitations, Chapter 1," "St Rupert's emails to the Corinthians"). The Elvi crash it by parachute, blooming over the black lawns "like a fleet of jewelled jellyfish," and walk in with their hair perfect. The vow is the character in one line — "With this cheque, I, Alexander Shakti, take you, Sarah Jacobson, as my lawfully contracted counterparty in holy joint venture" — answered by Sarah's flat "Yeah, me too." And Dunhilton, dragged there because Eloise overrode his three excuses, supplies the dread from the cheap seats: "Do you have any idea what you've just sanctified?"

**The reception and Halo's death.** The convergence works because everyone arrives as themselves. Alexander, hearing the High Elvis sing "The Wonder of You," cannot bear that the mondegreens are "wrong," seizes the microphone to correct them, and starts the brawl that gives Halo his opening. The fight that follows is well-built: Halo's safe-house fantasy (the altar, the waterproof sheet, the mirrors) makes the stakes concrete; the dropped shoe freezes him on his own professionalism long enough for Sarah to turn the tables; and the kill is a genuine accident — Hayley shoves him back into the elevator and three momenta converge on a blade Sarah is already holding. The deathbed reverses eight years in two lines — the thumb drive, "It's for you. It's time you knew," and "I'm sorry. I can't protect you anymore" — and the threat doesn't end so much as change hands, with the masked paramedic (Jones, by Chanta's gut) staring through the closing doors. Sarah's own coda refuses the melodrama the moment invites: she's wet herself, she's shaking, and what she thinks is "That mother-taxer deserves all he got."

**Gerry and Dunhilton.** Two comic instruments, and the register is power-theatre, not thriller. Dunhilton is grotesque cruelty played for laughs — reviewing the inauguration video at an axe-throwing parlour ("Executive Stress Relief Since the 19th Century") while live animals flee down a glass channel, ending on the therapy piglet and "Anyone for bacon?"; conducting genocide as household management ("no one misses a broken dishwasher or espresso machine. They just throw it out"). He is a humourless man rendered ridiculous, and the joke is consistently on him. Gerry is the opposite number who tolerates him and expects to outlast him: he throws his axe "with the kind of deliberate incompetence that suggested he'd calculated exactly where it would miss," needles his boss with a Zevon line the man can't catch ("And their hair was perfect"), and answers to "Your Serene Corporateness" while the prose tracks the clocking, unfooled interior underneath. When Dunhilton sets the deadline at Melbourne Cup Day, Gerry agrees — knowing that if the Elvi aren't dying on schedule, "he'd be dead instead." The discomfort is comic; the calculation is real.

**Jones's apophenia.** A single scene does a lot: Jones at a bar building a "Grand Unified Theory of Evil" out of cocktail napkins, eating the ones too dangerous to leave written (GYGES, DISC), arriving at "Elvian Jihad Man," diagnosed by the narrator (not by himself) as a man whose psychometric scores "slipped straight off the bell curve" into apophenia. It keeps him a credible menace while making him funny, and it quietly advances the real plot — the dissolved Department of Freedom, PAPER off its leash — through the lens of a paranoiac who happens, this time, to be roughly right.

## Where Part 4 Slackens

**Chanta's interior restates rather than develops.** The comedy around him in the studio escalates — the band's patience frays, Rusty and Pacer come to blows over song titles, the Dawkins fervour builds to a head injury and a mock resurrection — but Chanta's own inner monologue hits the same notes in nearly the same words each time he surfaces. He misses Sarah and Little Clinton; he believes they're his muse; he can't write without them; he finds the worship cringeworthy. That beat recurs across both recording scenes and again in the Dawkins scene, and because the language barely shifts, his thread reads as marking time even while the room around him moves. The breakthrough, when it comes, isn't his — the Elvi generate the Dawkins religion; Chanta mostly protests it. A blocked artist marking time is a fair subject, but the scenes dramatising the block would carry more if his self-understanding moved between them rather than resetting.

**Sarah's combat ability arrives unplanted.** She goes from no shown combat capacity to out-fighting a professional killer, and the prose is honest that even she can't account for it — "'I don't know,' Sarah said quietly, and it was the truth. She didn't know." The scene draws the debt cleanly: Halo himself wonders mid-fight whether she has "perfect reaction time as well as perfect health," whether she's "a woman at all." But nothing earlier in the Part — or visible to the reader before it — has shown her body doing something it shouldn't, so the competence reads as surprise rather than as a debt being paid. The fight wants a plant somewhere ahead of it, a lower-stakes moment where she moves faster or stronger than she should. (Flagged for retroactive planting.)

## The Threads

Part 4 advances or resolves a large slate of threads, and the management of them is sure-footed: rather than push everything at once, it runs the domestic and institutional threads through the middle and converges the active ones at the Gravity Well.

**Resolved:** Halo's hunt closes — Halo dead, the body retrieved by PAPER, the thumb drive passed to Chanta. The Scarlet Shower Surgeon arc, active since Part 1, ends here, though Hayley's identification dies with the body PAPER removes.

**Major advancement:** Sarah's biology (the combat ability surfaces, on top of the "invincible" health Kathy confirmed earlier); Eloise's empire (Project Kebab launched, Chip recruited, Random Allocation rigged, marriage executed); Alexander's romance (the wedding achieved — which means the novel can now take it from him).

**Steady pressure:** Jones and the disc (held mostly offstage, but the paramedic retrieval proves PAPER's reach); Gerry's virus (the genocide machinery set to Melbourne Cup Day); the Sages' prophecy (the Sages physically relocated to Sublime Point).

**Newly drawn:** the thumb drive (contents unknown, in Chanta's hands); the pattern Sarah recognises from the exploded lab "in dreams I've had... since" her pregnancy; the binary-disc heist that Chilo seeds with Johnny and that the five minarets and five-disc configuration keep pointing toward.

## Voice

The narrator sits further back here than in the early Parts. The intrusive worldbuilding gags are rarer; voice runs mostly through character action and dialogue, and the prose trusts the reader to carry the wedding and the death without an essay. What survives of the digressive mode — the MacArbitrage Futures riff at the reception, the Latter Day Capitalism liturgy — earns its place.

The character registers are wide-ranging, and no two sound alike. **Eloise** moves between corporate death-calculus, dramaturgical manipulation (the Three Sisters allegory), genuine-seeming vulnerability, and "Who's a good pussy cat?" without a seam. **Kathy** ranges from medical confidante to field operative to the flirt who tells Hayley "I'm sure you're fun even without chemical assistance." **Alexander** is sustained delusion — "With this cheque," the camera-turret instinct, claiming Halo's death on live television before Eloise conks him with the microphone ("You know this man?" / "Ah… no?" / "Good answer"). **Chip** arrives covert and institutional, a character defined by what he won't say. **Gerry** is the comic operator — performed deference over a clocking, calculating interior, with the dry private needle (the Zevon bait his boss can't catch) the only tell of his real stance. **Dunhilton** is grotesque comedy whose humourlessness is the thing being lampooned, the joke kept firmly on him. And **Jones** is menace-as-apophenia, dangerous and ridiculous in the same breath.

## Verdict

Part 4 is the commitment Part: Sarah chooses to marry into the Shakti machine and then chooses to run a piece of it; Hayley and Kathy choose Eloise; Chip defects; Gerry chooses the deadline; Halo chooses his moment. Its strengths are concentrated in the institutional architecture (Sublime Point, Project Kebab, the Chip recruitment), the character two-handers (the lookout, the negotiation, the inauguration's non-reunion), and the convergence at the Gravity Well that turns a reception into a death.

Its soft spots are two and specific. Chanta's interior restates itself across the studio scenes instead of developing, so his thread marks time while the room around him escalates. And Sarah's combat ability arrives without a plant, so the elevator fight reads as surprise rather than as a debt paid — the one place the Part draws on credit it hasn't yet established, and the one the author has flagged for retroactive planting.

Halo's death works less as the closing of a threat than as the reframing of a relationship: a man the reader took for a predator keeping his bait alive turns out, in two dying lines, to have been protecting him for eight years, and the novel declines to say which it was. Part 5 inherits a fully loaded field — Sarah married to Alexander but carrying another man's clone and now running the disc project; Chanta holding a dead friend's thumb drive; Hayley with the killer identified but no body to prove it; Jones behind a surgical mask; Eloise holding the weapons and the discs; Chip recruited and inside the compound, flagged a person of interest by his old agency and learning the disc tech from the inside; the Sages relocated with their suitcases; Gerry counting down to the first Tuesday in November.
