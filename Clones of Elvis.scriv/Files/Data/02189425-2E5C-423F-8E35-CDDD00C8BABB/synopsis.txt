1. Good news, badly received. Hardy is in a meeting he is enjoying — the landmarks campaign, the protection of culturally significant harbour frontage, money being spent on nothing at all. The lab's result comes up the line and is presented to the room as the finest thing the company has ever done. Everybody is delighted. Hardy is the only one who goes quiet, and the reason he goes quiet is that he is the only person in the room who owns anything. He thanks the presenter warmly, twice, and asks that the file not be copied.

2. He does not consult a scientist. He runs through who he could ask, and every one of them is wrong. Scientists will say yes. Doctors will say yes. Ministers leak. The board would want a briefing, and a briefing is a document with names on it. What he wants is an answer he can hold up, produced by people with no stake and no standing.

So he takes the finance department, and from the finance department he takes the juniors: a dozen accountants, economists and finance advisers who model things for a living and have never once been asked a question that mattered. They are told they have been selected. Several assume it is a promotion. One brings a folder of ideas he has been waiting four years to show somebody senior.

3. Down. The file cannot leave the site, so the modellers go to the file. Fifty metres, under the Pale. The chapter's only atmosphere is here and it should be brisk: the lift, the pressure doors, a corridor, a man in a good suit who does not know his own way and has to stop and read a sign. The juniors are impressed with themselves. Somebody takes a photograph and is asked not to.

4. The brief. A conference room. Bad light, a whiteboard, a jug of water. Hardy tells them he does not know any of their names and has declined to be told them: "You have been brought here precisely because I don't know you."

One question — what happens to the economy if disease ends. Seven hours. He does not say why seven. He is asked, and says "operational".

Then the questions from the floor, every one of them professional and every one beside the point: is this a memo or a deck; is there a template; are we assuming current tax settings; is the audience the board or the regulator; what happens if we need longer. Somebody asks about lunch. Hardy has thought harder about the catering order than about anything else that morning, and it is a very good one, and he is pleased to be able to say so.

5. Seven hours upstairs — and Hermann comes up. What Hardy does while they work: he has the drawings for the dome open on the table, and he is costing it. Cubic metres. He is extremely good at this. The contractors are on site because he sent them before he asked the question, and the reader gets to notice that on their own.

Then his friend comes up to see him. Hermann is delighted — Jamison, all this way, and obviously about the result. He has brought a printout. He wants to talk about scale-up, about a bigger trial, about whether they can afford to simply give it away, and whether they can afford not to. He talks about children. He mentions, in passing, the colleague running the field work, the one with the sick daughter, who has been all but living down here.

Hardy listens. He tells him it is magnificent, which he believes. He tells him to keep going. He shakes his hand. He does not say one untrue thing, and he lets him go back down.

6. The report. It arrives on time, because juniors deliver on time. It is competent. Executive summary, assumptions page, three scenarios, a risk register, an appendix nobody will ever read. The recommendation is one sentence: the miracle drug must be destroyed, all research must be destroyed, and all related research staff must be eliminated.

Nobody in that room wrote it in anger. They arrived at it, checked it, and are quietly pleased with it, the way you are pleased with a piece of work that came out clean.

7. The one who works it out. At the door, bag over the shoulder, halfway through asking about the car home, one of them does the arithmetic on all related research staff and stops walking.

And is embarrassed. And the others are mildly annoyed at him for being dramatic at the end of a long day.

That is the last thing that happens in that room in this book.

8. Hardy reads it and is relieved. Here is the mechanism of his heroism, put plainly and never commented on: he asked a question he already knew the answer to, and the answer has come back from outside himself, in writing, from people whose names he does not know. So it is not an opinion. It is a finding. He is complying with it.

He carries that for two hundred years and it never cracks once.

9. Procurement. The recommendation says destroy, so destroy gets costed. Incinerating a facility at that depth, against sealing it. The concrete comes in cheaper, and — this is the line the rest of the novel hangs off — it produces a receipt. A destroyed thing has to be certified destroyed by somebody who went down and looked. A sealed thing is certified by an invoice.

The order goes out as one line in a board paper and is executed the same week across a dozen installations by people who never met him and never asked. Nobody is evacuated. The chapter does not describe the sealing and does not go back down.

And that is how both viruses survive — intact, catalogued, dry, fifty metres down, waiting two centuries for a man with a drill and a deadline. Hardy destroys the cure by preserving it perfectly, and neither he nor anybody else ever notices.

10. The car, and the narrator. Hardy going up, deciding what he is. He has saved the entire world and no one will ever know, which he understands to be the curse of the true hero. He is sentimental about it. He may well be a little tearful, and the chapter should let him be, because that is the joke and it does not need pointing at.

Then the narrator takes the last word — briefly, in the voice that says our streets and in less decrepit universes. Nobody ever found out. Nobody ever will. He was right.
